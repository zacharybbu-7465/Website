Spenser's Comforting Doctrine of the Descent: Christ's Descent and the Mission of the Redcrosse Knight

Zachary Urdaneta

Introduction

In Book I of the Faerie Queene, Edmund Spenser (1552–1599) writes a riveting account of temptation, despair, and victory in the Christian life. He conveys his message through the character of the Redcrosse knight, Holinesse. The story functions, like all of Faerie Queene, as a multi-layered allegory of an epic founding of England and the Christian life. Spenser takes the familiar account of St. George, which impacted both Christian and English history, and uses it to teach the virtue of holiness. He seeks to spur the inhabitants of the British empire to greater virtue with nationalistic and Christian themes. However, Spenser's Faerie Queene goes beyond political allegory and motives and plumbs the depths of medieval and reformation theology. A thorough-going Anglican, infatuated with Queen Elizabeth I (1533–1603, reigned 1558–1603), he offers a tribute to her and the church of England through his writing. His historical and theological context plays an important role in understanding Book I of the Faerie Queene.

This paper will examine how Spenser uses dogmatics as the foundation and force behind his practical Christian exhortation. The Redcrosse knight finds hope after his encounter with Despair in the doctrine of Christ's descent into hell. At a time when the doctrine of the descent was in question, much as it is today, Spenser demonstrates how the Church of England's apostolic understanding of a local descent of Christ can be used to encourage and exhort the believer. The Redcrosse Knight receives encouragement from the New Covenant, including the descent of Christ, and he is inspired to imitate Christ in his descent, as he returns to slay the dragon and set the captives free.

The Redcrosse Knight's Battle with Despair

The section that Christians likely resonate with the most in Book I of Spenser's Faerie Queene is Canto 9: The Redcrosse knight's battle with Despayre. As Redcrosse travels, he sees a knight upon a horse galloping toward him. This knight is in a dreadful state of fear and is not properly armed. Redcrosse sees that "About his neck an hempen rope he weares" (Book I, Canto ix, stanza 22, line 7). Alarmed and confused by a knight wearing a noose around his neck, the Redcrosse knight questions him (23–24). Too embarrassed or stunned to speak at first, eventually, the knight says that he is fleeing from one seeking his life, "From him, that would haue forced me to dye" (26.2). The man he flees from is "That cursed wight... A man of hell, that calls himself Despayre," who preys on knights by "creeping close, as Snake in hidden weedes" (28.4–5, 8). Despayre extracts from the knights their sins and failures, and then encourages them to commit suicide, which the knight's companion does. This knight flees, and now warns the Redcrosse knight to avoid Despayre at all costs.

The Redcrosse knight does not heed the warning of Treuisan, but goes to encounter and try Despayre (32). The knight descends into his dark and abhorrent cave, where he finds a cursed, wretched man sitting on the ground (33–35). The Redcrosse knight confronts Despayre, saying that he must be held accountable for the suicide of the other knight, "What iustice can but iudge against thee right, With thine owne blood to price his blood, here shed in sight" (37.8–9). Despayre does not respond to this indictment well and argues that the death of the knight was true justice, for the one "who merites not to liue" should die and that it was "his own guiltie mind desruing death" that drove him to suicide (38.4, 6–7). Throughout Canto ix, Despayre alludes over and over to Romans 3:23, "The wages of sin is death" but never continues to the second half of the text, "but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord."1 The lover of death continues, saying of the dead knight, "He there does now enjoy eternall rest and happy ease... What if some little payne the passage hauve, that makes frayle flesh to feare the bitter wauve? Is not short payne well borne, that bringes long ease, And layes the soule to sleepe in quiet graue?" (40.1–2, 4–7). Despayre is attempting to show the benefit and virtue of suicide. He presents it as the way to eternal rest and happy ease. He describes it with language that one might use to describe the Christian life. He calls it a temporary pain, a momentary suffering that soon leads to blissful rest. As with the two knights prior, Despayre is trying to show the Redcrosse knight the feigned allure of suicide. Having already descended into the dark cave, the knight is tempted to descend even further to the grave.

The Redcrosse knight begins to respond by arguing that man's days are fixed, and he can neither lengthen nor shorten his life. However, his words are soon overtaken by Despayre who begins to transition tactics from showing the attraction of suicide to emphasizing the knight's guilt. He asks the knight, "Why then does thou, O man of sin, desire To draw thy dayes forth to their last degree? Is not the measure of thy sinfull hire High heaped vp with huge iniquitee, Against the day of wrath, to burden thee?" (46.1–5). Following Despayre's logic, there is no sense in prolonging your days to their full length when that would only pile your weight of sin even higher against you. It seems that within his words, there is an admission of an eternal state after death in which it would be less beneficial to have a higher stack of sins against you. However he earlier avoided any discussion of life after death earlier in calling suicide an eternal rest, and he now presses forward to discourage the knight over his sin with Duessa.

Using selective theology, Despayre reminds the knight of the truth of Romans 3:23 and 6:23 but preaches a distorted view of God's justice and wrath. He calls God just and asks, "Shall he thy sins vp in his knowledge fold, And guilty be of thine impietie?" (47.3–4). He argues that God cannot forgive the knight (folding his sins in his knowledge) for that would make him guilty of the knight's impiety. With no hope of forgiveness, there is no point in living for the knight. His sin with Duessa, if there is no forgiveness for it, will keep him from Una and his mission forever. Despayre then brings his argument to a close with these dreadful words,

Is not his lawe, Let euery sinner die:
Die shall all flesh? what then must needs be donne,
Is it not better to doe willinglie,
Death is the end of woes: die soone, O faries sonne.
(47.5–9).

If God condemns the sinner, Despayre reasons, the sinner ought to take his own life before he accumulates more guilt or God takes his life for him. If death truly is the end of woes, then the Redcrosse knight should want it to come quickly. If escape cannot be found through forgiveness, perhaps it can come through death.

The knight recalls his crimes and experiences a deep awareness of his sin. Despayre capitalizes on the change in the knight of Holynesse and shows him the anguish of souls in hell (49). Seeing eternal punishment "thoroughly him dismaid, That nought but death before his eies he saw, and euer burning wrath before him laid" (50.1–3). The knight understands the agony of eternal punishment. He is not deceived by what he sees, he does not see a false reality. However, he wrongly views himself as condemned to that punishment. The villain Despayre then brings all manner of weapons, hoping that the knight will take them and send himself to that punishment. When the knight hesitates, he puts a dagger into his hand (51.1–3). He takes up the dagger to end his life, but Una runs to his rescue, "Out of his hand she snatcht the cursed knife, and threw it to the ground" (52.4–5). She rebukes the knight and asks, "What meanest thou by this reproachfull strife?" (52.7). The strife is reproachful, because he is striving with himself. The knight is battling his own mind and his own sin. Yet his way of ending the strife is by giving in to Despayre and attempting to take his own life. This strife with himself is reproachful because it was not the battle he was called to "With that firemouthed Dragon, horrible and bright" (52.9). To bring him out of the cave, Una then reminds him, "In heauenly mercies hast thou not a part? Why shouldst thou then despeire, that chosen art?" (53.4–5). The knight has partaken of heavenly mercies, he is one of God's chosen. He is not destined for the anguish of hell but for the joys of heaven.

The Redcrosse knight descended, but he did not descend to set the captives free. He descended into the hellish cave of Despayre. This descent was done in his own strength, as he thought he could defeat Despayre. He was captured in his mind by an overwhelming sense of his guilt before God. He forgot his salvation; he forgot his freedom. He sees the anguish of souls in hell. He forgot the one who descended into hell for them. So, Una leads the knight in an ascent to see the joys of heaven. He ascends to the House of Holiness so that he can be prepared to descend a second time.

The Holy House and the Healing of the Gospel

The Redcrosse knight failed to conquer Despayre in his own strength. He forgot the forgiveness of the gospel as he saw those who were suffering in hell for their sins. The inhabitants of the Holy House and Hospital remind him of the gospel and spiritually restore him from his near-death encounter with despair. Una leads him to the lady of the house, Caelia. Here the reader finally learns Una's mission through the words of Caelia, "Most virtuous virgin borne of heuenly berth, That to redeeme thy woefull parents head, From tyrans rage, and euer-dying dread, Hast wandred through the world now long a day" (x.8.3–6). Una is on a quest to redeem her parents from a tyrant's rage. "From tyrans rage" serves a double function, as the tyrant's rage is both what her parents are to be redeemed from, and what causes Una to wander. She needs to save her parents from the tyrant, but she is forced to wander to escape that very tyrant. The Dame Caelia greets Una and the Redcrosse knight and two of her children, Fidelia and Speranza proceed to welcome them as well. After the guests rest, Fidelia teaches Redcrosse "her heauenly learning" and "celestiall discipline, And opened his dull eyes, that light mote in them shine" (18.5, 8–9). She teaches him with her sacred book and heavenly documents, and as she does, "the faithfull knight now grew in little space, By hearing her, and by her sisters lore" (21.1–2). The knight learns faithfulness, from the woman who represents faithfulness, by her teaching of God's word and obedience. He is reminded of the truth he previously forgot and learns how to put it into practice.

Although the knight learns truth from Fidelia, he has not yet attained to perfect knowledge or obedience. He learns to loathe his former life of sin, but as he does, he loathes the whole world and his mortal life, because of his immense guilt over his sin (21). So the next sister, Speranza (hope) "gaue him comfort sweet And taught him how to take assured hold Vpon her siluer anchor" (22.1–3). Spenser alludes to Hebrews 6:18–19, which describes "the hope which we have as the anchor of the soul, both secure and confirmed and entering into the veil." The hope enters into the veil because it is brought there by Christ who entered on our behalf (Heb 6:20). So Speranza teaches the Redcrosse knight to cling to the hope we have, a hope which is a secure anchor because it has been confirmed by Christ who entered into the veil as our high priest. Yet the knight's grief over his sin continued, as "the cause and root of all his ill, Inward corruption, and infected sin, Not purg'd nor healed, behind remained still, And festring sore did ranckle yet within" (25.1–4). His indwelling sin must be driven out of him so that he can be freed from the full effects of his battle with Despayre. He was visited by Penaunce, Remorse, and Repentance, but his grief remains self-focused. He does not move beyond grief over his sin, so his torment continues. He lives in self-loathing and self-hatred, as if he is still in the cave of Despayre instead of the House of Holiness. For lasting change, he needs Repentaunce and Charissa.

Trew Repentaunce comes to Una as Charissa (love) at last gives birth. At this time, with "his torment well withstood, In that sad house of Penaunce, where his spring Had past the paines of hell, and long enduring night" (32.7–9), the knight is instructed by Charissa. She shows him how to properly love what is good, and hate what is evil. Instead of loathing himself, he learns to loath what God loathes (33). She calls Mercy to instruct him "that he should neuer fall In all his waies through this wide worldes wauve, That Mercy in the end his righteous soule might saue" (34.7–9). It is Mercy who leads the knight to the "holy Hospitall" (36.1), where he will learn the mission he has been prepared for.

The hospital contains seven bed-men (nurses) who have "vowed all Their life to seruice of high heauens king" (36.3–4). These nurses serve in a holy hospital whose gates stay open to any needy, poor, and weary traveler. Each of the seven represents an aspect of Christ's character or life, serving in the same way that he did. They both care for visitors and model how they ought to live. They teach godly character to the knight. During his time there, he learns virtue and "so perfect he became, That from the first vnto the last degree, His mortall life he learned had to frame in holy righteousnesse, without rebuke or blame" (45.6–9). Each of the seven evidently has a role in instructing the Redcrosse knight, but the fourth nurse in particular plays an important part in the story.

The fourth bed-man was appointed for "Poore prisoners to relieue with gratious ayd, And Captiues to redeem with price of bras" (40.2–3). He reflects Christ's heart for the prisoners and afflicted. He relieves them with aid, as Christ cares for his people, and he even redeems them from prison with "price of bras" (payment of money). As Jesus loves the imprisoned and redeems them with his own blood, so this fourth nurse cares for and redeems the prisoners. He reflects Christ's work on the cross in his monetary redemption of the captives. Next, Spenser carefully notes the kind of prisoners this nurse sets free. Like the knight himself, the prisoners the fourth bed-man cares for are guilty. He redeems them, "though they faulty were, yet well he wayd, That God to vs forgiueth euery howre" (40.5–6). Christ redeems the prisoners by paying for their sins with his blood. His blood is poured out for the forgiveness of sins (Matt 26:28). The prisoners set free by Christ are guilty. They are held in bonds for their sins, serving a just penalty. In like manner, the fourth nurse is caring for and redeeming guilty prisoners. They are faulty, and yet he considers how God forgives our sins through Christ, so the nurse does the same for those who are imprisoned. He lives in accordance with Paul's instruction, "Be kind to one another, affectionate, forgiving each other just as God in Christ forgave you" (Eph 4:32). The forgiveness shown to the nurse is poured out in turn to those he cares for. God has forgiven the nurse for his sins, so he forgives the prisoners.

God forgives every one of our horrors, but he did far more. The nurse not only forgives the prisoners he sets them free, and he does so because of Christ. Jesus forgives, but "Much more then that, why they in bands were layd, And he that harrowed hell with heauie stowre, The faulty soules from thence brought to his heauenly bowre" (40.7–9). The mission of the fourth nurse has a deep theological foundation. He ministers to the prisoners because Christ not only cared for the prisoners during his earthly ministry, but set them free in his death. Spenser confesses the biblical and apostolic doctrine that Christ descended into hell, announced the damnation of the devil and his foes and led his own to paradise. The saints of old were, as it were, laid in bands. Christ harrowed hell, stealing his people from that place, bringing those faulty souls to his heavenly bowre, his heavenly garden. This doctrine, although taught from the earliest days of the church in the Apostle's Creed, had fallen out of favor in the days of Spenser. In books 10 and 11 of Faerie Queene, Spenser articulates the doctrine of the descent in the context of the fourth nurse, and then shows how this becomes the foundation of the Redcrosse Knight's mission. Christ's descent, although neglected or marginalized by many of the reformers, was a focal point of the Church of England and the Thirty-nine Articles of Religion. Spenser, as a thorough Anglican, articulates a geographic view of the descent, which he then uses to encourage and motivate the believer. To understand the thrust of Spenser's theology, the descent of Christ must be examined from a historical perspective.

The most famous reference to the descent of Christ on Holy Saturday is contained in the Apostle's Creed, κατελθόντα εἰς ᾅδου, τῇ τρίτῃ ἡμέρᾳ ἀναστάντα ἐκ νεκρῶν, "He descended into hell, on the third day he rose from the dead."2 According to tradition, each line of the Creed was written by one of the original twelve apostles.3 Whether or not directly written by the apostles themselves, the Creed is a faithful summary of apostolic teaching, and has been passed down and confessed by each generation of the church. The most controversial clause of this basic summary of orthodoxy is this sixth line, traditionally ascribed to the apostle Thomas. Many renditions of the creed have removed or altered this clause, as the church has drifted away from the traditional view of Christ's descent. Calvin rebuked such notions of removing this line, as he wrote, "If any persons have scruples about admitting this article into the Creed, it will soon be made plain how important it is to the sum of our redemption: if it is left out, much of the benefit of Christ's death will be lost."4 Although an essential part of the Apostle's Creed, different views arose in the church as to how to understand the Creed and texts such as Psalm 16, Ephesians 4:8–10, and 1 Peter 3:19. Spenser wrote at a time when the Reformed tradition, following Calvin, drifted away from a geographic understanding of Holy Saturday. The Westminster Confession and the Three Forms of Unity all articulate an understanding of Christ's body being buried and his soul suffering the wrath of hell on the cross but ascending into heaven upon his death.5 However, the Thirty-nine Articles are often overlooked in an analysis of the Reformation and historic and modern views of the descent. At a time when the rest of the Reformation (save Vermigli) moved to a metaphorical understanding of "he descended into hell" as Christ's suffering and burial, the Church of England retained a geographic interpretation.

The third of the Thirty-nine Articles of Religion reads, "As Christ died for us, and was buried, so also it is to be believed, that he went down into Hell." The Anglican Church affirmed a plain, geographic reading of the Apostle's Creed. Peter Heylyn (1599–1662) was an author, and member of the Anglican Church, who was born the year Spenser died, and wrote about the formation of the Thirty-nine Articles and the Anglican beliefs at the time of Spenser. In his work, Theologia Veterum, or, The Summe of Christian Theologie, which is a commentary on the Apostle's Creed, Heylyn devotes chapter 8 to an exposition of "He descended into hell" and chapter 9 to a historical examination of the clause, and a defense of the Church of England's understanding of it. He notes that in 1562, "Queen Elizabeth... caused her clergy to be called together in a Synodical way, to the intent they might agree upon a body or Book of Articles touching true religion."6 The Faerie Queene herself is credited with pushing for the ratification of the Articles as the official dogma of the church of England. Spenser's confessional beliefs were ultimately ratified as a result of Elizabeth's influence. Thus, the Articles are essential to the theology of Spenser and even relate to the Queene of his writing. Heylyn notes that this was not a debated topic within the Anglican church, as he pontificates on the common acceptance and mandatory subscription to the whole of the Articles, including the third on Christ's descent.7 The Homilies of the Church of England likewise include and elaborate on the confession, "He descended into hell." The Anglican Church in the time of Spenser wholeheartedly confessed the doctrine of Christ's descent. So, in writing on Christ's descent, Spenser is not out of step with the Church of England. He is confessing their understanding of Christ's geographic descent into hell, in contrast with the rest of the Reformation.

It is clear that the Anglican Church and Spenser confessed with the Apostle's Creed, that Christ descended into hell. The Creed and the Articles both pair the descent and the resurrection, and both are right to be understood together. Christ's descent is traditionally known as the harrowing of hell, which is the description Spenser adopts (x.40.8). Hell is harrowed as Christ descends and takes captive souls to paradise. Paul writes, quoting from Psalm 68:18, that, "When he ascended on high, he led captive a host of captives, giving gifts to people. Now this, 'He ascended,' what is it except that he also descended into the lower parts of the earth?" (Eph 4:8–9). Christ's taking captives captive is viewed on one hand as the freeing of prisoners, and on the other as robbing hell. From the perspective of the prisoners, it is a joyous victory, but from the perspective of hell, it signals defeat as its captives are taken captive. This is expressed in Christ's taunt to hell's demons, as Peter writes, "He heralded to the spirits in prison" (1 Pet 3:19). Christ's death, descent, and resurrection are the beginning of the defeat of death itself. He, "has been raised from the dead ones, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Cor 15:20), and he reigns with those come to life in him, the first resurrection (Rev 20:4–5). Finally, "Death is the last enemy to be abolished" (1 Cor 15:25). At the last resurrection, the rest of the dead are raised (Rev 20:11–13), and death itself is ultimately killed, as it is thrown into the second death, the fiery lake (20:14).

The descent of Christ then is integral to his entire mission. By descending to the grave and taking captive his captive people, he demonstrates his power over death. He descends in order to ascend with his prisoners. He ascends with his freed captives into paradise, and as he does so, the great red dragon is thrown down (Rev 12:7–17). The bruised dragon, along with death, has his defeat begun at Christ's death, descent, resurrection, and ascension. In his descent, Christ demonstrates that his work on the cross is complete, that he frees the prisoners, that he has defeated and will ultimately abolish death and evil, and the dragon now flies with a bruised head.

Spenser, as a devout Anglican and dutiful subject of the Faerie Queene, Elizabeth I, accepts the geographic descent of Christ as expressed in the Anglican understanding of the Apostle's Creed. The fourth nurse follows Christ's example of caring for the prisoners, because Christ "harrowd hell with heauie stowre, The faulty soules from thence brought to his heauenly bowre" (x.40.8–9). Jesus robbed hell of its captives and having paid the price for the redemption of the souls in hell, he brought them up to his heavenly garden. Spenser does not present an allegorical understanding of Christ's descent. He does not follow many of the Reformed of his day in confessing a descent that is merely the burial of Christ's body and hellish anguish of his soul on the cross. He believed with the Anglican Church that Christ descended into hell, and that because of his death, burial, descent, and resurrection, he was able to proclaim his victory over hell's forces and take captive the host of captives to paradise. They were guilty prisoners, but he paid their price of redemption so that he could descend and take them in his ascent.

Each of the nurses of the Holy Hospital serves as an encouragement and example to the Redcrosse knight. The descent of Christ, as reflected in the ministry of the fourth nurse instructs the Redcrosse knight in a deeply personal way. The knight's battle with Despayre nearly ended in suicide because he was held captive by the weight of his guilt. Christ's descent shows that he paid the price to redeem guilty sinners, and that there is no condemnation for them, because he freed them from the prison. The believer does not need to fear the eternal judgment of hell, because Christ descended into hell for him. The descent of Christ indicates that his work on the cross was finished, and his resurrection shows that death and sin have no hold on the believer. As Jesus ascended with his host of captives, so all who are in him will be raised on the last day. The Redcrosse knight nearly ended his life because he was so captivated by the vision of souls suffering in hell. Despayre showed him "painted in a table plaine, The damned ghosts, that doe in torments waile, And thousand feends that doe them endless paine with fire and brimstone, which for euer shall remaine" (ix.49.6–9). The Redcrosse knight was driven to the point of death because he only saw the souls in hell, but not Christ who descended into hell for him. As John Andrew Bryant aptly writes, "The question isn't whether hell exists. The question is whether Christ has descended into hell. The question is whether hell is a place He will go to get us."8 Hell is real, and the hellish anguish of souls the knight saw was real. However, the reality of hell does not mean the knight will perish. He can be lifted up from despair within his soul by looking to Christ who descended into hell for him. Christ descended to set the prisoners free. Redcrosse must remember that he is not a captive suffering in hell, but a captive of sin that Christ died, descended, and ascended to set free. The fourth nurse, by his reflection of Christ's work, instructs the knight to look to the risen Christ. However, the knight does more than simply look to Christ, he follows in his steps.

The Redcrosse Knight's Descent and Victory

Redcrosse is comforted from his fight with Despayre by Christ who descended into hell. Now, he likewise will descend in order to set the captives free. As noted above, immediately after saving him from suicide, Una asked the knight, "Is this the battaile, which thou vauntest to fight, With that fire-mouthed Dragon, horrible and bright?" (ix.52.8–9). The knight's ultimate battle is not with Despayre, but with the dragon. The dragon has captured Una's parents, and she has been forced into exile to escape his wrath (x.9.4–6). However, the Redcrosse knight needed to be restored from his fight with Despayre and instructed in his proper mission. Following the fourth nurse, the knight prepares to imitate Christ in his descent and setting the prisoners free. After leaving the Holy Hospital, the knight ascends to see the heavenly Jerusalem. He is so captivated by its beauty and glory that he does not want to return to the world of sin and trouble any longer (x.63). However, he is called to fulfill his mission defeating the dragon and setting the captives free. He finds himself (x.68) and returns to Una. He then descends back to the world to fight the dragon. Christ left the heavenly Jerusalem and descended in order to defeat the dragon and set the prisoners free, and the Redcrosse knight must do the same.

Una takes the knight back to her native land and says to him, "The sparke of noble corage now awake, And striue your excellent selfe to excel" (xi.2.6–7). She shows him "The brazen towre in which my parents deare For dread of that huge feend emprisoned be" (3.2–3). Una's parents are imprisoned by the dragon, so Redcrosse must wage war with the dragon. He fights, not only for the sake of defeating the evil dragon, but in order to set the captives free. Christ descended into hell, not only to proclaim victory over the power of hell, but to take his captives into heaven with him. Redcrosse is able to fight the dragon now that he has been healed from his battle with Despayre. He descends and fights knowing that Christ descended into hell for him. He fights the dragon for three days, and on the third day, he runs his sword through the dragon's mouth (53). The prisoners, Una's parents, the King and Queen of Eden are set free. Like Christ, Redcrosse has descended, defeated the dragon, and set the captives free.

Conclusion

Spenser holds to a geographic view of the descent, and demonstrates the practical implications of the doctrine. The Redcrosse knight nearly ended his life in despair over the anguish of seeing souls in hell and fearing that he might become one of them. The doctrine of Christ's descent shows Christians that they do not need to fear hell because Christ has already harrowed it. The knight realizes that he is not one of the souls in hell, but one of the prisoners that Christ has set free. He does not need to fear that he will go to hell, but he can be confident that Christ went to hell for him. The doctrine of the descent is not merely an interesting topic of debate. It has profound implications for the Christian life. The knight not only receives comfort from the descent, but he sees his calling in it as well. He follows Christ's pattern by descending to fight the dragon and set the captives free. The Christian should reflect Christ's forgiveness and salvation of guilty prisoners. God uses his people today in the task of proclaiming the gospel as he rescues souls from the power of darkness and transfers them to the kingdom of his beloved son (Col 1:13). Every time God transfers another person to his kingdom, it is as if Christ is robbing hell yet again. The believer joins in this work of descent and setting free by spreading the gospel of the Christ who died, descended, and ascended for his sake. Moreover, he can take comfort, as the Redcrosse knight did, in the descent of Christ. The believer knows he has been set free from the chains of hell because Christ took the captives captive in his ascent. Christ descended to set the prisoners free so that they would continue the work of robbing hell until the day of his return.
