Introduction

The Epistle to the Hebrews is a sermonic exhortation to enter the Sabbath-rest by focusing on our superior high priest. In his two main directives, the author urges believers to "Consider the apostle and high priest of our confession—Jesus" (Heb 3:1) and to be "fixing our eyes on the beginner and finisher of our faith—Jesus" (12:2).1 The first exhortation comes at the end of a beautiful declaration of Christ's superiority over every created being (1:1-3:6), while the second follows an examination of faith throughout redemptive history (11). Christians are to trust in Jesus, their great high priest, and rest in his intercession and completion of their faith. Jesus is the Messianic priest-king of Psalm 110 and the object of the faith of the righteous man in Habakkuk 2:4, which serve as the two anchor texts for Hebrews. The reward of the Christian who perseveres by faith in the high priest is God's Sabbath rest. In Hebrews 3-4, the author expounds on Psalm 95, where David reflected on the wilderness generation. While Israel of old failed to enter the rest because of unbelief, the Israel of God must press on to enter his remaining rest by faith in the high priest.

Context

Hebrews 1:1-3:6 focuses on the superiority of Christ. The opening of chapter three begins to move to the author's next great theme, as he opens with a transition and nominative of direct address Ὅθεν, ἀδελφοὶ ἅγιοι ("so then, my beloved brethren").2 He demonstrates Christ's superiority one more time in vv. 2-5 to reinforce his primary imperative, κατανοήσατε ("consider"), from v. 1. His transition is completed in v. 6, as he moves from Christ's faithfulness as a son over his house, to our faithfulness as Christ's house. We are his house, true believers, "if we hold fast (κατάσχωμεν) our confidence and the boast of our hope" (3:6). Those who do not hold fast are like the wilderness generation (3:7-19) and are thus not God's house. Those who do hold fast will enter God's Sabbath rest (4:1-13) and be his house for eternity. The author repeats the main subjunctive in v. 3:14, declaring that "we have become participants in Christ if we hold fast (κατάσχωμεν) the beginning of our assurance firm until the end." The difference between entering and being barred from God's rest is whether the individual holds fast to his hope. The hearts of Israel fainted in the wilderness and they forsook their confidence in God. They tested him instead of confessing him. The recipients of Christ's rest are those who hold fast to their confidence, hope, and assurance (3:6, 14).

The author shows what will happen if his readers do not hold fast—they will fail to enter the rest like the wilderness generation (3:7-19). Westfall helpfully summarizes that while 3:1-4:1 focuses on the readers' identity and danger in hardening their hearts to Jesus' message, 4:1-13 focuses on the reality of the readers' opportunity in responding positively to Jesus' message.3 In 4:1-13, the author exhorts the readers to enter the remaining sabbath rest through faith, for the message of God is a double-edged sword, and they must decide how to respond to it (4:1-13). Hebrews 4:1-13 is about the Christian's response to Psalm 95 and the disobedience of the wilderness generation. The question is: Will they be unbelieving and fall short (3:19) or believe and enter the rest (4:3)?

Outline

Hebrews 4:1-13 opens with a subjunctive declaration, supported by two indicative statements (4:1-3a). The author next dives into a theology of the Sabbath, proving that a Sabbath rest remains for God's people (4:9) because the rest existed from the world's foundation (4:3b-4), the rest endures for some to fail and some to enter (4:5-6a), disobedience bars entry (4:6b), the rest is for today (4:7), and Joshua spoke of it (4:8). He then defines the Sabbath rest as a rest from works (4:10) and arrives at his main exhortation: we must press on to enter into that rest (4:11). Finally, he adds an additional reason to press on by two statements about God's message—it is a double-edged sword, and the message is given to us (4:12-13).

Opening exhortation (4:1-3a)

The opening call of Hebrews 4:1-13 returns the reader to the present age. Having exposited Psalm 95 and declaring that the wilderness generation, "did not enter because of unbelief" (3:19), the author brings application to his readers with a hortatory subjunctive, "Therefore, we must be fearful" (Φοβηθῶμεν οὖν).4 As Westfall comments, while the following unit in 4:1-13 is set apart structurally by the occurrence of the hortatory subjunctive, it has many cohesive ties with 3:1-4:1.5 The failure of the wilderness generation to enter the Sabbath rest is a cause for alarm among lackadaisical Christians. He has already applied the warning of Psalm 95 to the church, writing, "Today if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion" (3:15). The readers must not be hard of heart, but fearful, for if some fell before, some may fall again. The new covenant community are partakers of Christ only if they hold fast to him (3:14). If they do not hold fast, they too may fail to enter because of unbelief (3:19). This is cause for a holy fear. Calvin pastorally comments that the fear here recommended is not that which shakes the confidence of faith, but such as fills us with concern that we grow not torpid with indifference.6 The fear the author commands should stir the reader to enter God's rest, and there is good reason for this fear.

Reason: The possibility of not entering the rest (4:1b)

The new covenant community is called to fear since a promise of entering the rest remains, but some may fall short of it. The reader should take some encouragement, as "a promise remains of entering into his rest." Within the genitive absolute construction, καταλειπομένης ἐπαγγελίας εἰσελθεῖν εἰς τὴν κατάπαυσιν αὐτοῦ, the author uses the present participle καταλειπομένης to emphasize the present and continuing nature of the promise.7 The promise is still in effect—God's people will enter his rest. Since the promise remains, the possibility of falling short of the promise also remains. The infinitive ὑστερηκέναι is in the perfect tense,8 to mark not only a present or past defeat but an abiding failure.9 Those who fall short of the promise while it remains are left in that state. Kleinig says the picture is of a lagging traveler who does not keep up with his companions and so fails to reach the destination of their journey.10 The picture applies well to both the wilderness generation and the generations of the new covenant.11

One message (4:2a)

The promise of entering God's rest remains which demonstrates that Canaan was not the final resting place. The hope for the Hebrews is not Canaan, but God's eternal Sabbath rest as the author will extrapolate in this text. Bruce writes that the meaning of that "rest" was not exhausted by the earthly Canaan which was entered by the Israelites of the generation which had grown up to manhood in the wilderness; the spiritual counterpart of the earthly Canaan is the goal of the people of God today.12 The rest (κατάπαυσιν) which the wilderness generation was barred from (3:11, 18) can and must be entered by the covenant community today (4:1). Israel fell short of the earthly κατάπαυσιν but the church must not fall short of the eternal κατάπαυσιν. Israel and the church have the same good news proclaimed to them (4:2). The passive participle, εὐηγγελισμένοι ("good news proclaimed") is the message (ὁ λόγος) of entering God's rest.13 The wilderness generation was called to enter the rest, as David called the Israel of his day in Psalm 95. As Westcott writes, the Israelites in the wilderness and the first Christians were in the same position.14 Where Israel failed to enter God's rest, the church must be fearful and not fall short of the promise. Those in the church who do not enter it while the promise remains are like the generation dying in the wilderness, outside of the promised land. Adam stood outside of Eden, and Israel stood outside of Canaan, so the readers are encouraged to persevere and enter the promised rest. The difference between Israel and the church is not the message, but the hearers.

Unbelieving hearers (4:2b)

The church and Israel both had good news proclaimed (εὐηγγελισμένοι) to them, "but the message that was heard did not profit" (ἀλλʼ οὐκ ὠφέλησεν ὁ λόγος τῆς ἀκοῆς). The message, the promise of entering the rest (4:1) was not profitable, but not because of any flaws in the message itself. The message did not profit "the ones who have not been united with those who heard in faith" (4:2). The Greek text is rather ambiguous and has a textual question. The NA 28/UBS 5 and the THGNT read ὁ λόγος τῆς ἀκοῆς ἐκείνους μὴ συγκεκερασμένους τῇ πίστει τοῖς ἀκούσασιν. The reading μὴ συγκεκερασμένους15 ("the ones who have not been united") takes the participle as referencing a generation who were not in unison with the faithful. There are several alternatives to this reading, of which the most common is συγκεκερασμένος,16 which would modify ὁ λόγος. Thus, the text would say that the word was not united with faith in its hearers. The accusative plural συγκεκερασμένους is the more difficult reading, which along with manuscript evidence, is why the critical texts adopt it. Metzger comments that συγκεκερασμένους is supported by early and diverse testimony representing both the Alexandrian and the Western types, and as the more difficult reading it would naturally have been altered to the easier nominative singular.17 Taking the reading συγκεκερασμένους, the reference is to the wilderness generation not being united with the ones who heard in faith.

Believing hearers (4:3a)

The believing hearers are "the ones who heard with faith" (τῇ πίστει τοῖς ἀκούσασιν). The substantival participial τοῖς ἀκούσασιν is paired with τῇ πίστει to show how the message was heard. The ones who fell short of entering God's rest, the wilderness generation described in 3:7-19, were not united with the ones who heard with faith. The identity of the believing hearers in v. 2 is sometimes speculated to be Moses, or Joshua and Caleb.18 However, the text makes their identity clear in the next phrase, "For we—the ones who believe—enter into that rest" (εἰσερχόμεθα γὰρ εἰς [τὴν] κατάπαυσιν οἱ πιστεύσαντες). The generation of old is not united (μὴ συγκεκερασμένους) with the ones who heard with faith (τῇ πίστει τοῖς ἀκούσασιν), namely the ones who believe (οἱ πιστεύσαντες). Attridge aptly summarizes that the author is not saying that the ancient Israelites were not united to the faithful remnant, Caleb and Joshua, who heard the message; rather, he says they were not united to "us" who do, he hopes, listen to the message.19 The wilderness generation is not united with the believing generation of the new covenant. As the author concluded at the end of chapter 3, "they were not able to enter because of unbelief" (3:19). Their unbelief (ἀπιστίαν; 3:19) is contrasted with those who heard in faith (τῇ πίστει; 4:2) and are thus called "the believing ones" (οἱ πιστεύσαντες; 4:3). Those who do not believe fail to enter the rest, and so they have not been united with the ones who heard with faith (4:2). The believers "enter into the rest" while the unbelievers do not, as God swore in his wrath, "they will never enter into my rest" (4:3). The unbelievers and believers are not united, because one enters the rest, while the other falls short. The author next describes these two destiny verses as part of his exhortation from 4:1 to not fall short of the rest.

Theology of the rest (4:3b-10)

After urging the readers, the ones who believe, to enter the rest, the author then lays out a theology of the remaining Sabbath rest for God's people. Those who were not united with the believers were barred from the rest, but those who are fearful and persevere in faith will receive entry.

Edenic rest (4:3b-4)

The rest that Israel fell short of, but those who believe will enter, is Edenic. The Lord swore in Psalm 95, as quoted in v. 3, that the wilderness generation would never enter into his rest. Now the verse concludes by showing that he was not to blame for their failed entry. They were barred from the gate to rest, "and yet the works have been finished from the foundation of the world" (καίτοι τῶν ἔργων ἀπὸ καταβολῆς κόσμου γενηθέντων). "And yet" translates καίτοι (και + τοι) which is a concessive conjunction and serves to introduce the theological point made in v. 4.20 Following the conjunction, the author uses a genitive absolute construction, with γενηθέντων as the participle modifying τῶν ἔργων. The genitive absolute is functioning temporally, emphasizing that the works have been finished from the foundation of the world. According to Kleinig, the article before εργων implies possession, "his works" — this genitive absolute concedes that entry into God's rest has been possible ever since the completion of God's works.21 The works of God have been finished from the foundation of the world. As Lane puts it, God's rest consists in the completion of his works, and consequently his rest has been in existence since the foundation of the world.22 The unbelieving generation failed to enter the rest despite God's works being completed. The completion of those works is then explained in the next verse.

The author uses an indefinite introduction to a familiar text, "For he says somewhere concerning the seventh, in this way," (εἴρηκεν γάρ που περὶ τῆς ἑβδόμης οὕτως). This indefinite way of introducing a quotation is unique to here in the NT but is used in other ancient Greek texts.23 The quotation, "and God rested on the seventh day from all his works" (Καὶ κατέπαυσεν ὁ θεὸς ἐν τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τῇ ἑβδόμῃ ἀπὸ πάντων τῶν ἔργων αὐτοῦ), from Genesis 2:2 serves to demonstrate the truth of the concession—that his works have been finished from the foundation of the world (4:3). The author follows the LXX in his quotation, except for inserting ὁ θεὸς to make the subject explicit. God rested on the seventh day in order to establish that seventh day as Sabbath. On the seventh day, God rested from all his works, creating a perpetual state of rest. God finished creation by creating a Sabbath-rest, and so the divine rest was prepared: God himself had entered into it, though it still remained that his people should share it according to his purpose.24 However, those people do not enter that rest.

Future rest (4:5-6a)

The author reintroduces the Psalm 95:11 quotation in v. 5, "And in this again, they shall never enter into my rest" (καὶ ἐν τούτῳ πάλιν, Εἰ εἰσελεύσονται εἰς τὴν κατάπαυσίν μου). God's rest began from the foundation of the world (4:3), on the seventh day of creation (4:4). The wilderness generation did not fail to enter because of any fault of the rest. The rest predated them and continued to exist after them. Bruce summarizes that it was not because the "rest" of God was not yet available that the wilderness generation of Israelites failed to enter into it; it had been available ever since creation's work was ended.25 The homiletic force of vv. 3-5 is clear: Israel did not fail to enter the rest because of God or the rest itself. The rest has been available from the end of the creation week. Their failure to enter is because God swore that they should never enter (4:3, 5). The reason for his oath was already made explicit, "They did not enter because of unbelief" (3:19). Allen writes that although God's works were completed and the rest available, yet they did not enter into it — the expectation was that they should have entered.26 The sole reason for Israel's failure to enter was their unbelief. Yet their unbelief does not make the rest unattainable for all. The purpose of Hebrews 3:7-4:13 is to urge the readers to not be like the wilderness generation but to press on to enter the true and better land of Canaan.27 This is seen in the nature of the rest.

As demonstrated above, vv. 3-4 show that God's rest is intimately tied to the Sabbath. God's rest was established on the seventh day, existing from the foundation of the world. The land of Canaan was a resting ground on the way, but it was not the ultimate place of rest. Kleinig writes that this rest is not the promised land or the temple as God's place of rest; it is God's primordial time and place of rest, which he established when he founded the world and which he entered when he had completed his creative works on the seventh day.28 God's rest existed before the promised land, before the temple, before the church. It is the perfection of creation, established before the fall, and it is the destiny of those who believe (4:3). The author of Hebrews returns to Eden because the rest that lay before the wilderness generation, and that lies before the new covenant church, is the seventh-day rest of the creation.29 The rest was established at creation and continues throughout the history of creation. This rest is still future for the readers of the epistle. Israel failed to enter and was barred from ever entering (4:3, 5). The need for a prohibition shows that the possibility of entrance still stands. The rest was before and is after their failure. It is an abiding eternal rest, and the author uses Israel's failure to urge his readers to press on.

The author begins one sentence that spans vv. 6-7 by tying it to the previous, "Therefore, since it remains for some to enter it" (ἐπεὶ οὖν ἀπολείπεται τινὰς εἰσελθεῖν εἰς αὐτήν). He uses the postpositive οὖν to show that his next statement is a result of the two quotations in vv. 4-5. As a result of God resting on the seventh day and swearing that the wilderness generation failed to enter his rest, it remains for some to enter it. The Sabbath predated Israel's failure, so it is not done away with by their falling short. The rest has been established, so it remains for some to enter it. The indefinite pronoun τινὰς shows that this entry goes beyond the readers of the epistle. In accordance with v. 3 ("we who believe enter into that rest") the author uses τινὰς to show that a variety of people, and a great number of them, will enter God's rest. Israel's unbelief does not negate the existence of a future entry.

Past failure to enter (4:6b)

Functioning as a marker of cause, the conjunction ἐπεὶ that opens v. 6 governs both halves of the verse. The two phrases, "it remains for some to enter into it and the ones who formerly had good news preached to them did not enter because of disobedience" are the dual cause of the author's following declaration in v. 7. The second phrase under ἐπεὶ returns to the theme of 3:19. There is a rest that has been established, and it remains for some to enter it, but there was a group that fell short before. This group formerly had good news preached to them (οἱ πρότερον εὐαγγελισθέντες). The aorist passive participle εὐαγγελισθέντες30 was used in the perfect in v. 2 (εὐηγγελισμένοι) to describe both the wilderness generation and the church. Here the aorist participle modifies the verb εἰσῆλθον ("they did not enter").31 They were preached to, but they did not enter the rest. There is no room for finding fault with the rest, for it was already established (4:3-4), nor with a lack of a message, for the message was preached and heard (4:2, 6). The reason for that generation's falling short was their unbelief (ἀπείθειαν). The two reasons are paralleled with the preposition and the alpha-privative, διʼ ἀπιστίαν (3:19) and διʼ ἀπείθειαν (4:6). The rest was held from that generation because of their unbelief and disobedience. In this way, they serve as a warning to the church.

Israel was disqualified from the physical land of rest because of their unbelief and disobedience. Their refusal to enter the rest is a crucial part of the author's caution to not fall short of the rest. Unbelieving Israel being kept from the promised land is a foreshadowing of how all who are unbelieving and disobedient will be kept from God's eternal rest. Kleinig captures this relation well: God's disqualification of the faithless from entry into the promised land, which was gained by those who believed God's Word of promise, prefigures the present exclusion of unbelievers and the entry of the faithful into his heavenly rest.32 The wilderness generation did not believe, and so fell short of the land of physical rest. Their unbelief likewise kept them from God's eternal rest, of which the land of Canaan was only a type. In like manner, all who are unbelieving and disobedient will fall short of God's rest. Those who fail to enter among the readers of the epistle will find themselves in the same place as the generation who died in the wilderness.

Many fail to enter God's rest because of unbelief and disobedience (3:19; 4:6). Yet God did not establish his rest in vain. Since (ἐπεὶ) it remains for some to enter and those who had the message preached did not enter, there must be a new generation of participants in God's rest. The door to God's rest did not close in the wilderness. Since the rest is awaiting entry, God appoints a new day.

Present hope of entry (4:7)

Since it remains for some to enter, and Israel did not enter because of disobedience, God appoints a new today of entry. For the third time (3:7, 15) the author quotes from Psalm 95:7. This time, the quotation is subordinate to ἐπεὶ οὖν (4:6) and is introduced, "Again he appoints some day, 'Today,'" (πάλιν τινὰ ὁρίζει ἡμέραν, Σήμερον). "Again" (πάλιν) is subsequent to the first appointing of a day of rest. In the thematic context, the prior appointment would be Moses calling Israel to enter the promised land. In the literary context, the prior appointment would likely be the establishment of the Sabbath day (4:3-4). Regardless, the point stands that by the word "Today" in Psalm 95, God is again appointing a day of entry into his rest. He does so, "saying by David after a long time, just as it has been said before" (ἐν Δαυὶδ λέγων μετὰ τοσοῦτον χρόνον, καθὼς προείρηται). The message of Psalm 95 is not just a condemnation, as it was so quoted in 3:7-11, but a message of hope. Allen captures the hope in v. 7, writing that the promise of rest was still available in the time of David, hundreds of years after the exodus generation's failure to enter the rest.33 A new day of entry has been appointed, so David declared, "Today if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts" (Σήμερον ἐὰν τῆς φωνῆς αὐτοῦ ἀκούσητε, μὴ σκληρύνητε τὰς καρδίας ὑμῶν). The wilderness generation hardened their hearts against God and failed to enter the physical rest. David used them as an example to warn the generation of his day to not fail to enter God's spiritual rest. According to Bruce, a later generation of Israelites was warned by the psalmist not to follow the bad example of their ancestors' refusal to listen to God, lest disaster should overtake them in turn; and now a still later generation has the same warning impressed upon it by the writer to the Hebrews.34 The author of Hebrews takes the warning that David uses in his day and appropriates it for his readers. However, David's words in the context of the eternal rest in ch. 4 raise a question: If Israel was kept from the physical rest under Moses, but the second generation did enter the promised land under Joshua, why does David still say "Today"?

Future entry (4:8)

Here the author brings out a logical inference from Psalm 95 that he has not attested to in his prior quotations of the text (3:7, 15). David's exhortation to not be like the wilderness generation is not merely a command to not be hard-hearted or rebellious in general. David specifically has God's rest in view, a rest beyond the land of Canaan. After the death of the wilderness generation, their children entered the land of Canaan under the leadership of Joshua. Why then does David issue a warning? The author of Hebrews explains, "For if Joshua had caused them to rest, he would not have spoken concerning another day after these things" (εἰ γὰρ αὐτοὺς Ἰησοῦς κατέπαυσεν, οὐκ ἂν περὶ ἄλλης ἐλάλει μετὰ ταῦτα ἡμέρας). Verse 8 opens with a first-class conditional statement, marked by ει plus the indicative (κατέπαυσεν), assuming the reality of the condition. If Joshua had actually brought Israel into the final resting place, then David would not speak of another day. Therefore, Joshua did not ultimately cause Israel to rest (κατέπαυσεν).35 He brought them into the land of Canaan, but that was not the ultimate resting place.

The author makes the relation between the earthly and heavenly rests clear here in v. 8. Bruce says of the readers that the heavenly rest which lies before them is the counterpart to the earthly Canaan which was the goal of the Israelites.36 Theologically, Calvin says the land of Canaan was not otherwise so much valued except for this reason, because it was an image and a symbol of the spiritual inheritance.37 Canaan is not the rest the author of Hebrews is exhorting his readers to enter, but rather, as Kistemaker words it, the rest of which God speaks is a spiritual rest and has much greater significance than living safely in Canaan.38 The land of Canaan was a true inheritance, a true resting place. However, Israel was meant to look beyond the land of Canaan and press on toward God's eternal Sabbath rest. Although in a place of rest, Calvin says, in a sense they ought not to have rested as though they had attained to the summit of their wishes, but on the contrary to meditate on what was spiritual as by it suggested.39 The Israel of David's day was living in that land of rest, so Psalm 95 is urging them to look beyond it and meditate on what was spiritual. The author of Hebrews does the same for his audience.

The unbelieving and disobedient wilderness generation did not enter the earthly rest. The conquest generation did not inherit the final rest. The generations following failed to look beyond the land of Canaan to the heavenly rest. So, the author summarizes his theology of the rest (4:3b-10) with a bold declaration: the rest remains.

Remaining rest (4:9-10)

The high point of the author's theological treatise of God's rest occurs in verse 9, as marked by the inferential conjunction ἄρα.40 The author draws an additional conclusion from the exposition of Ps 95:7b-11 in Heb 3:7-19 in the light of Gen 2:2-3.41 Since the wilderness generation failed to enter the rest and Joshua could not give rest to the conquest generation, "So a Sabbath-rest remains for the people of God" (ἄρα ἀπολείπεται σαββατισμὸς τῷ λαῷ τοῦ θεοῦ). The syntax of v. 9 is simple, the conclusion of 4:3b-8 is that a σαββατισμὸς remains for God's people. The word σαββατισμὸς does not exist prior to this in classical Greek, occurs only here in the NT, and commentators agree that it is a term coined by the author. It is a nominalization of the verb σαββατίζω, which appears in seven texts in the LXX.42 To this point the author of Hebrews, following Psalm 95:7, has used the noun κατάπαυσις (and its verbal form καταπαύω) to refer to God's rest and act of resting. This term was even used for the creation of the original Sabbath rest in v. 4. Now in v. 9, the author coins a term to nuance the rest he is describing. God rested, creating the Sabbath rest, and the readers are encouraged to enter his rest. Now the author uses σαββατισμὸς to clarify that the rest God's people enter is his Sabbath-rest. O'Brien helpfully summarizes that the term sabbatismos is not synonymous with katapausis, but explains what takes place in God's resting place, namely, an eternal, festive sabbath celebration.43 The church has been exhorted to enter God's rest, and in v. 9, the author declares that the rest remains and is an eternal Sabbath. Entering the σαββατισμὸς is the believer's participation in God's own rest.44 Those who enter God's Sabbath-rest enjoy fellowship with God and celebrate the Sabbath in his presence. God, in a sense, dwells in this Sabbath, having entered it on the seventh day (4:3b-4). The believer enters into God's dwelling place and in v. 10, enjoys the same quality of rest.

The believer enters into God's rest and rests in the same way as God does. The author describes the quality of their rest, "For the one entering into his rest also rested from his works, as God did from his own" (ὁ γὰρ εἰσελθὼν εἰς τὴν κατάπαυσιν αὐτοῦ καὶ αὐτὸς κατέπαυσεν ἀπὸ τῶν ἔργων αὐτοῦ ὥσπερ ἀπὸ τῶν ἰδίων ὁ θεός). The rest remains for the people of God, so they enter into it. The aorist participle εἰσελθὼν ("entering") is substantival,45 describing one of God's people who faithfully (3:19) and obediently (4:6) enters his Sabbath-rest. The aorist participle is modified by the aorist verb κατέπαυσεν ("rested"), indicating concurrent action.46 The one who enters God's rest rests from his works. Wallace categorizes κατέπαυσεν as a proleptic aorist, describing an event that is not yet past as though it were already completed.47 The final entry into God's rest is future, but it is so certain that the believer will enter, that the author speaks as though he has already rested.

The believer's rest from his works corresponds with God's. The σαββατισμὸς of v. 9 is not just entry into the place of God's rest, but a quality of rest like that of God's. The one entering God's rest is in a state of rest from his works, "as God from his own" (ὥσπερ ἀπὸ τῶν ἰδίων ὁ θεός). This conclusion is illustrated by the linguistic parallels between verses 4 and 10.

God's Rest (4:4)Καὶ κατέπαυσεν ὁ θεὸς…ἀπὸ πάντων τῶν ἔργων αὐτοῦ
Believer's Rest (4:10)καὶ αὐτὸς κατέπαυσεν ἀπὸ τῶν ἔργων αὐτοῦ

The Sabbath-rest that remains for the believer (4:9) is not just entry into the place of God's rest (4:3) but entry into the quality of rest that God enjoys. The one who enters will enjoy the rest that is necessary for the festivity and praise of a Sabbath celebration.48 As God rested from his works on the seventh day, so the believer will rest from his works when he enters God's seventh-day rest. O'Brien comments on this parallel: whoever enters this eschatological rest will have completed their service on earth, just as God finished his work after creation — the parallelism between the two clauses is striking, the rest is not only God's, it is also the believer's.49 The believer enters God's rest and takes part in it. As God rests, so do all who enter into his rest.

The believer rests by entering into God's Sabbath-rest and resting from his works. However, the author does not specify what works the believer is resting from. The simplest reading indicates that the believer entering into the eschatological rest of God will have rested from their works from this life. As seen previously, the rest remaining for believers is Edenic, the seventh-day rest of God. In God's eternal Sabbath-rest, the believer enjoys rest from the works of this world. One might object that prelapsarian Adam did in fact work in the garden (Gen 2:15). However, there is a specific dimension to working that is counted as part of the curse (Gen 3:17b-19). Whereas food was abundantly available before, with man merely working and guarding the garden, now after the fall, man suffers and strains himself to grow food from the dust. The curse inflicted on Adam is focused on works. He is exiled from the garden (Gen 3:23-24) and sent to work for his food. In Adam's exile, mankind is exiled; in his work, mankind works; in his death, mankind dies. Works are a part of the curse in the exile of man from the garden. However, the message of Hebrews 4:1-13 is that those who believe will enter God's seventh day Sabbath-rest—that they will return from exile. Adam's exile is reversed as believers enter God's resting place. And as one enters God's Sabbath-rest, he "also rested from his works as God did from his own" (4:10). The believer's rest from works is the reversal of Adam's curse. God rested from his works after the work of creation was finished. The believer rests from his works as the new creation dawns. This contributes to the author's main theological point—that a Sabbath-rest remains. So Leschert writes that the people of God, from all indications, have never rested from their works as God did from his; since God's people have never experienced the rest that was promised to them, the possibility of entering it must remain open in order for the promise to be valid.50 With a rich theology of the Sabbath-resting-place laid out, the author finally turns to his main exhortation.

Main exhortation: Enter into that rest (4:11)

After reviewing those who fell short of the rest, understanding the nature of God's rest, and learning what entrance into his rest is like, the reader is now exhorted to enter that rest (4:11) and is given two reasons to enter it (4:12-13). The text reads, "Therefore, let us press on to enter into that rest so that no one will fall into the same pattern of disobedience," (σπουδάσωμεν οὖν εἰσελθεῖν εἰς ἐκείνην τὴν κατάπαυσιν, ἵνα μὴ ἐν τῷ αὐτῷ τις ὑποδείγματι πέσῃ τῆς ἀπειθείας). This exhortation is grammatically subordinate to the opening of this section (4:1), Φοβηθῶμεν οὖν ("Therefore, we must be fearful"), however, the only command given in the first is to be fearful lest someone falls short (ὑστερηκέναι). The author now gives the full exhortation, in light of the doctrine of God's Sabbath-rest (4:3b-10). His main exhortation in 4:1-11 is to "press on to enter into that rest." "Press on," translating σπουδάσωμεν,51 a verb of motion that contains a sense of urgency.52 Working with εἰσελθὼν in v.10, σπουδάσωμεν shows that we must not only enter, but be diligent in our entry. A casual attitude toward entering God's rest will leave you outside the gates, falling short of his sabbath (4:1). The readers, along with the author, must zealously strive for the entrance to God's rest. They are to be like the person described in 4:10—the enterer (εἰσελθὼν)—who enters God's rest and rests from his works. The author shifts from a general axiomatic statement in 4:10 to a related, specific command in 4:11 (cf. vv. 5-6). The two subjunctive exhortations, Φοβηθῶμεν οὖν (4:1) and σπουδάσωμεν οὖν (4:11) are framed by the theology of the Sabbath-rest (4:3b-10). The church is to have an attitude comprised of healthy fear of falling short and eagerness to enter. They are to do so, because of the nature of God's rest.

The author uses an anaphoric construction, ἐκείνην τὴν κατάπαυσιν ("enter into that rest") to describe the rest the believers are pressing forward to enter. The demonstrative ἐκείνην combines with the anaphoric use of the article τὴν to refer to the κατάπαυσιν spoken of earlier in the passage. The κατάπαυσιν was defined in v. 9 as a σαββατισμὸς (Sabbath-rest) and is God's place of rest which he entered into perpetually on the seventh day (4:4), resting from his works (4:10). It is that rest which the author urges himself and his readers to press on to enter.53 The believer who perseveres to the end will enter, not just the land of Canaan, not just the Israel of David's day, but the Edenic Sabbath-rest of God. The author urges the readers to enter it, for God has appointed a certain day, "Today" (4:7). Kleinig helpfully summarizes the theological force behind the author's exhortation: God provides the Sabbath-rest as the eschatological goal for his created cosmos, and it has been permanently available since then, so God's disqualification of the faithless from entry into the promised land prefigures the present exclusion of unbelievers and the entry of the faithful into his heavenly rest.54 The theological treatise on God's Sabbath-rest (4:3b-10) serves to emphasize the urgency for believers to enter that rest.

God's rest has been available, but as Israel failed to enter (3:19; 4:6) and the conquest generation failed to look ahead to the true rest (4:8), the church is in danger of falling "into the same pattern of disobedience" (4:11). Here in the author's main exhortation, the readers face the same dangers as Moses' followers (3:7-19) but they are also offered the same opportunity on the basis of Ps. 95.55 The followers of Moses and Joshua had opportunity to enter the physical and spiritual land of rest. They fell short because of disobedience (4:6). Now the followers of the author of Hebrews have the same opportunity to enter the spiritual land of rest since God appoints today as the day of entry (4:7). Even though the wilderness and conquest generations failed to enter, the author of Hebrews can still urge his readers to press on because "there remains a Sabbath-rest for the people of God" (4:9). The theology of rest (4:3b-10) gives credence and legitimacy to the text's main exhortation (4:11). Westfall even goes so far to say that 4:1-13 answers a question raised about the legitimacy of commanding the readers to enter the rest that the Israelites in the wilderness generation failed to enter.56 The author uses a theology of the Sabbath to demonstrate the legitimacy and urgency of his call to enter. He can call the church to enter God's rest in good conscience, because it remains, and he can urge them to do so emphatically, because of the final phrase of v. 11.

The church is urged to press on, "so that no one will fall into the same pattern of disobedience" (ἵνα μὴ ἐν τῷ αὐτῷ τις ὑποδείγματι πέσῃ τῆς ἀπειθείας). The pattern of ἀπειθείας, "disobedience," is the pattern of Israel's sin, when they did not enter διʼ ἀπείθειαν, "because of disobedience" (4:6). Those who do not enter God's rest will fall like Israel. The corpses of those who did not enter God's rest "fell (ἔπεσεν) in the wilderness" (3:17).57 Likewise, those who do not press on will fall (πέσῃ) into the same pattern of disobedience.58 The author makes an explicit connection between the disobedience of those who fail to enter in the church and Israel who failed to enter because of disobedience. He also makes an allusion to his earlier statement, that as the corpses of the disobedient ones fell (πιπτω) in the wilderness, so those who do not enter God's rest will fall (πιπτω) into their pattern of disobedience. As the corpses fell, so the dead unbelievers who do not heed the exhortation will fall into disobedience.59 Their corpses will then fall in like manner. Westcott analyzes that those who so fall become, in their punishment, an example like that offered by the Jews in the Wilderness, an example of the fatal consequences of disobedience fitted to alarm others.60 The church must press on to enter God's rest, or else they too will fall and become an example like that of the wilderness generation. Those who fall will be cut down by the sword of God's message.

Reasons to enter that rest (4:12-13)

The author of Hebrews closes his section exhorting the church to enter God's rest by describing the judgment of his message should they fail to do so. These verses describe two reasons God's people should be fearful (4:1), press on, and not fall short (4:11). First, the message of God is a double-edged sword, and second, all are laid bare before God and his sword of judgment.

The message is a sword (4:12)

Believers must press on to enter God's rest "Because the message of God is living and energetic and sharper than any double-edged sword" (Ζῶν γὰρ ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ καὶ ἐνεργὴς καὶ τομώτερος ὑπὲρ πᾶσαν μάχαιραν δίστομον). For the second time in this subsection, the author mentions God's λόγος. Previously the λόγος which was heard did not profit those who failed to enter God's rest (4:2). That same message becomes a double-edged sword to all who do not press on to enter his rest. This is not a dead message, but one that is living (Ζῶν) and energetic (ἐνεργὴς). The message of judgment that God brings on those who heard, but reject his message is living. It is not a dead message, but one that will judge those who do not enter God's rest.61 It is an energetic, or powerful, message of judgment.

The two adjectives "living and energetic" show the efficaciousness of God's message. Returning to 4:2, the message that was heard was unprofitable. However, the reason that message was unprofitable was not because of a flaw in the message itself, but in the unbelieving and disobedient hearers. In the same way here in v. 12, the author defends God's message by calling it living and energetic. The previous hearers did not fail to enter his rest because of the message, for it is living and energetic, but because they refused to believe and obey it. The living and energetic message then becomes a message of judgment for them.

The message of God does not cut gently. The author of Hebrews uses a graphic metaphor to describe the double-edged sword's judgment. God's message is "piercing until the division of soul and spirit, of both joints and marrow" (διϊκνούμενος ἄχρι μερισμοῦ ψυχῆς καὶ πνεύματος, ἁρμῶν τε καὶ μυελῶν). The sword does not cut, but pierces (διϊκνούμενος).62 In this imagery, the sword does not wound, it kills. The sword pierces until, or as far as (ἄχρι) the divisions between the soul and the spirit, and between joints and marrow. The sense is that it pierces up to the separation, in themselves, of soul and spirit, and of joints and marrow.63 While these phrases raise questions about the makeup of the soul and dichotomy versus trichotomy, these are not the issues the author of Hebrews has in mind. The author is continuing his image of a sword and describes the divisions (whether or not they exist) between soul and spirit and joints and marrow. The doublet, according to Smillie, rather than opposing the physical to the spiritual aspects of an individual, is more likely a literary device linking the two pairs together.64 Similarly, Bruce rightly cautions that it would be precarious to draw any conclusions from these words about our author's psychology, nor is it necessary to understand them in the sense of the Pauline distinction between soul and spirit.65 The author's purpose is not to delve into the nature of the inner man, but to show that God's message of judgment pierces to the depths of a person, such that it is able to "judge the thoughts and intentions of the heart" (4:12). The message is not merely external, condemning those who do not enter God's rest. It pierces to the heart, condemning those who are unbelieving (3:19) and disobedient (4:6). Those who do not enter God's rest are slain by the same message they refused to heed, like a certain Moabite king.

Although not normally described as an allusion, or even cited at all, the description of a double-edged sword piercing the inner parts of the soul of an unbeliever is drawn from, and a direct reference to, the slaying of Eglon in Judges 3. The exact phrase μάχαιραν δίστομον only occurs here in the NT, but in three locations in the LXX: Judges 3:16; Psalm 149:6; and Proverbs 5:4.66 Bruce states that the expression "a two-edged sword" occurs a few times in the Old Testament, but there is no particular reference here to any one of its Old Testament occurrences.67 Unlike Ehud, Bruce misses the mark by glossing over Judges 3. Other writers like Smillie reference the text and even compare the weapon in the two passages. However, he stops short of calling it an allusion, or saying that the author of Hebrews is drawing on the story.68 This author contends that when the texts of Hebrews 4:12 and Judges 3:15-30 (LXX) are examined together, it becomes clear that Hebrews explicitly alludes to the account of Ehud. The text draws the imagery for the phrase μάχαιραν δίστομον (double-edged sword) from Ehud's sword, as he stabs the king of Moab.

The other Old Testament quotations and allusions in the book of Hebrews are all taken from the LXX, and Hebrews 4:12 is no different. The LXX text of Judges 3:15-30 is a tight translation of the Hebrew text and may be examined to understand the narrative itself and the allusion to it in Hebrews 4:12.69 As noted above, Judges 3:16 is one of the three locations where the phrase μάχαιραν δίστομον is used in the LXX. The author of Judges writes that Ehud made for himself a double-edged sword, καὶ ἐποίησεν ἑαυτῷ Αωδ μάχαιραν δίστομον. Etymologically, the sword is "two-mouthed" (στομα δυο), a literal translation of the Hebrew. Ehud makes a μάχαιραν δίστομον for himself to execute God's judgment on Eglon, king of Moab. He conceals the double-edged sword as he brings Israel's tribute to the king. To make the king rise from his seat, Ehud says, "I have a message from God for you," Λόγος θεοῦ μοι πρὸς σέ (3:20). Ehud says nothing further after this, and when the king draws near, Ehud takes the sword (μάχαιραν) and thrusts it into Eglon's belly (3:21). The message of God (Λόγος θεοῦ) is the sword (μάχαιραν).70 Next, the author of Judges graphically details the reach of the sword, as Ehud thrusted it into Eglon's belly, and moreover even the hilt went in after the blade and the fat closed in upon the blade, for Ehud did not draw out the sword from his belly (3:21b-22). The text makes careful note of the depth of Ehud's stabbing. The double-edged sword goes into the inner being of Eglon. All three elements in Judges, the double-edged sword, the sword being God's message, and the depth of the stabbing, are appropriated by the author of Hebrews.

In his description of God's message of judgment upon those who fail to enter his rest, the author of Hebrews lexically connects his warning to three components of the Ehud narrative. Hebrews says that the message of God (ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ) is sharper than any double-edged sword (μάχαιραν δίστομον). Ehud had a sharp double-edged sword to pierce the fat belly of Eglon, but the message of God is even sharper. The comparison between the double-edged sword and God's message does not originate with the author of Hebrews. Within the Ehud narrative, the author of Judges makes it clear that the message from God is the sword the king is stabbed with (Judg 3:20-21). The message that is sharper than a double-edged sword pierces to the division of soul and spirit, joints and marrow. This is reflective of the description of the deep stabbing in 3:21b-22.71 The key components of the description of God's message in Hebrews 4:12 all come from Judges 3. The author takes the narrative, which already shows that the message from God is a double-edged sword, and alludes to it to indicate the seriousness of God's judgment. Those who fail to enter God's rest, who do not press on, who fall into the same pattern of disobedience (Heb 4:11) will be judged by the message of God, just like Eglon (4:12). This often-overlooked OT allusion serves to reinforce and intensify the message of Hebrews 4:1-13.

The message is for us (4:13)

The church must decide how to respond to the exhortation to enter God's Sabbath-rest. The author of Hebrews concludes his exhortation with a personal and practical note in v. 13. Lest the readers think that God's double-edged sword will not pierce them, the author warns that God sees all people, that they too are liable to judgment should they fall short of his rest, and that they must decide how to respond to his message. The reason this message of judgment can pierce anyone is "there is no invisible creature before him" (οὐκ ἔστιν κτίσις ἀφανὴς ἐνώπιον αὐτοῦ). The antecedent of αὐτοῦ must be θεοῦ (4:12), so the author of Hebrews is declaring that no one, man or beast, or anything in creation can escape God's sight. "Invisible" translates ἀφανὴς, which only occurs here as a noun, and is simply the root φαινω with the alpha privative. The reason no one is invisible before God is indicated by the phrase following δὲ, "all are naked and laid bare before his eyes" (πάντα δὲ γυμνὰ καὶ τετραχηλισμένα τοῖς ὀφθαλμοῖς αὐτοῦ). Mankind, although clothed, is in a sense the same before God as Adam and Eve were in the garden—naked and exposed before him. Adam and Eve could not cover their sins with garments of fig leaves or hide in the bushes, and neither can any of their descendants. All creatures are not only naked (γυμνὰ), but they are also laid bare (τετραχηλισμένα).72 A hapax, occurring only here in the NT or LXX, τετραχηλισμένα has a debated meaning. It likely stems from the noun τραχηλος, which means neck throughout the NT. If so, the author of Hebrews would be referring to creatures being grabbed by the neck, i.e. brought directly before God's eyes. The verb is a perfect participle, indicating that all creatures are in the state of being before God's eyes, hence the idiomatic translation, "laid bare" captures the sense of τετραχηλισμένα. Not only are creatures not invisible, their necks have been dragged as it were, directly into God's line of vision.

God himself will judge those who do not enter his rest. The message of faith, if rendered unprofitable through disobedience and unbelief (4:2) will cut the unrepentant like a double-edged sword. Since no one can escape God's sight, all who do not enter his rest will be judged. Here in v. 13, the author shows that exposure to the word of scripture entails exposure to God himself.73 Those who hear God's message have been brought before God himself. He will be certain to judge all who do not respond with fear (4:1) and press forward to enter his rest (4:11). Therefore, the author concludes this Sabbath-rest warning with a final word of exhortation, "for whom the message is to us" (πρὸς ὃν ἡμῖν ὁ λόγος). This phrase is ambiguous and has led to much debate over its meaning. The subject ὁ λόγος, has been mentioned in 4:2 ("the message which was heard") and 4:12. In both cases, it refers to God's message: that those who do not believe will never enter his rest and that the church must fearfully press on to enter it. This message will be a piercing sword to all who do not heed it. It makes the most sense then, for this third use of ὁ λόγος to also refer to God's message. As Smillie writes, it is uncharacteristic of a writer that he would, after carefully delineating the many and various qualities of ὁ λόγος in v. 12, bring the pericope to a close using ὁ λόγος as the very last word in figurative expression with a meaning having little to do with the principal theme of the discourse.74 There is no reason to assume that ὁ λόγος has any different meaning in 4:13, than in the rest of the text.

Some translations and authors see a rare meaning of ὁ λόγος in v. 13 and render it "to whom [God] we must give an account." However, as Smillie and others point out, three conditions are always present when λόγος means "account." It must be paired with a form of διδωμι or δοσις (to give, a gift), it is ordinarily anarthrous, and it must be in the accusative case.75 None of these three conditions are met in Hebrews 4:13, as ὁ λόγος is not paired with any verb, it is definite, and it is in the nominative case. The author of Hebrews actually uses λογος to refer to an account in 13:17, where he writes "as those who must give an account" (ὡς λόγον ἀποδώσοντες), and collocates λογος with a form of διδωμι and uses it in the accusative. The meaning of λογος as account, instead of a message, is readily apparent in Hebrews 13:17 from the structure of the phrase itself and the context. This is not the case in 4:13. The author of Hebrews uses ὁ λόγος three times in 4:1-13, and he refers to God's message each time.

The best way to understand the final phrase of Hebrews 4:1-13 is in reference to the obligation of the message's hearers. All creatures are naked and laid bare before God, and his message is for them. The double-edged sword has been set before God's creation, and they must decide what to do with it. The choice to respond in faith or unbelief was given to the wilderness generation, and they chose to stay outside the promised land. The conquest generation under Joshua was given the opportunity and obligation to look for the true rest, beyond the physical land of Canaan. They too failed to enter God's eternal, Sabbath-rest. Yet all hope is not lost, for God has set today, as the day for entry into his rest. The message is clear: be fearful and press on to enter his rest (4:1, 11). This message is a piercing, double-edged sword, and lies before all who hear it, as they lie naked and exposed before God. Those who do not heed the message will find themselves pierced through, like Eglon the king of Moab. Those who heed the warning of Hebrews 4:1-13 and press on, entering God's rest, will find rest from their works. They will enter God's blessed, eternal Sabbath-rest. Aquinas writes, "If God created man for eternal beatitude, since to His own image and likeness He created him, therefore He prepared a rest for him… And therefore it remains that some are to enter in."76 The Sabbath-rest remains for the people of God. His people are to press on, persevering through the grace of Christ's high-priestly ministry. The road of the Christian life leads to an eternal seventh day. Those who enter God's Sabbath-rest will dwell with him, freed from works, enjoying eternal, blessed rest in the new Eden.
