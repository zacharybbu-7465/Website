Introduction

John Milton's graphic depiction of Satan, Sin, and Death in Book II of Paradise Lost is carefully crafted to defend a biblical understanding of the nature of evil.1 His writing is grounded in Augustinian thought traced through church history, especially in Anselm, Aquinas, and the Reformers. Included in Augustine's two greatest works, Confessions and City of God, is his view of evil's essence and origin. In his autobiography, Confessions, he explains how he came to understand evil through Neo-Platonism, theology, and his own spiritual journey. In his theological overview of redemptive history, City of God, he explains evil by unpacking the fall of the devil and his angels. These works of Augustine have served as a fountain for Christian thought to the present day, especially in relation to the problem of evil.2 Augustine's presentation of a Christian, Neo-Platonic metaphysic has impacted every Western thinker. The Reformation, which Milton viewed as the second greatest event in Church history after the life of Christ, was a return to the theology of the early church and to Augustine as the preeminent church father.3 In light of Augustine's impact, it is no surprise that Milton, a Reformed Puritan scholar, used Augustine's theology and metaphysics in his own thinking. Augustine's metaphysical presentation of the origin of evil, the fall of the Devil, and the righteousness of God align well with Milton's goal in Paradise Lost, to "assert Eternal Providence, And justifie the wayes of God to men" (1.25–26).4 To present a theodicy, a justification of God to man, one must answer the overarching problem of evil. Milton does this broadly throughout Paradise Lost, and nuances his argument, particularly at the end of Book II, by examining evil's origin and substance (or lack thereof). It is possible to draw out Milton's understanding of evil from his narrative and dialogue, and in doing so, one can see the Augustinian theology behind it. I will structure my argument based on an exposition of Sin's speech to Satan, and then examine Augustine and his successors' doctrine of the origin and substance of evil and the fall, supported with additional sections from Paradise Lost. Milton presents a strong defense of the holiness of God against the dark backdrop of evil, and he does so in a poetic narrative, following Augustine's theology.

The Miltonic Origin of Evil

The first two Books of Paradise Lost describe the Devil and his angels after their fall from heaven. The devils hold a conference, Pandemonium, and debate their next course of action. Satan, the leader of the fall and now of the fallen angels, convinces them to seek the downfall of God's new creation: man and woman. In a twisted parody of the Son being sent to the world to bring salvation to mankind, the Devil volunteers to find Adam and Eve and tempt them. As he flies to the Gates of Hell, he encounters two beings in front of its Gates. Milton describes the first as,

Woman to the waste, and fair,
But ended foul in many a scaly fould
Voluminous and vast, a Serpent arm'd
With mortal sting. (2.650–53)

She appears to be a woman to the middle, with "Hell Hounds" (654) around her waist, and a serpent tail from the waist down. The other being, although less revolting, is equally strange, with Milton introducing it as,

The other shape,
If shape it might be call'd that shape had none
Distinguishable in member, joynt, or limb,
Or substance might be call'd that shadow seem'd,
For each seem'd either; black it stood as Night. (666–70)

This shapeless shape held a "dreadful Dart" (672) and "the likeness of a Kingly Crown had on" (673). This miserable being is Death itself (788), presented as king of Hell by the crown upon his head. The Devil, viewing himself as the true king of Hell, immediately begins arguing with Death. In response, "the Goblin" (688) replies by accurately identifying Satan as "that Traitor Angel... Who first broke peace in Heav'n" (689–90). Naturally, the Devil takes offense at this, and "Incenst with indignation" (707), they begin fighting. Their conflict only comes to an end through the intervention of Sin, as "the Snakie Sorceress... with hideous outcry rush'd between" (724–26). This intercession by Sin begins her dialogue with Satan, where she recounts her origin, and defends Augustine's approach to the problem of evil.

The dialogue between Satan and Sin encompasses several hundred lines, but the heart of Milton's theodicy is seen in her first two speeches to Satan. She addresses the Devil with words parodying the crucifixion, asking, "O Father, what intends thy hand...against thy only Son?" (727–28).5 Sin referring to Satan as "Father" and his opponent, Death, as "Son" bothers Satan, and he questions her statements, saying "I know thee not, nor ever saw till now," perhaps parodying Peter's denial of Christ. So Sin, "the Portress of Hell Gate" (746) responds to Satan by recounting her birth and the birth of Death. First, she takes offense at Satan's revulsion to her, saying that she was "once deemed so fair" (749). Next, she describes the place of her birth in Heaven. Raphael recounts these events in Book V, where he describes the Father's presentation of the Son in all his glory, and Satan's conception of malice (5.666). As the hosts of heaven stood to honor the Son, Sin says to Satan that she "with thee combin'd In bold conspiracy against Heav'ns King" (2.750–51). Yet where did Sin come from, could she also be an angel created by God? She explains that she was not created by God, but birthed by Satan:

All on a sudden miserable pain
Surprisd thee, dim thine eyes, and dizzie swumm
In darkness, while thy head flames thick and fast
Threw forth, till on the left side op'ning wide,
Likest to thee in shape and count'nance bright,
Then shining Heav'nly fair, a Goddess arm'd
Out of thy head I sprung. (752–758)

Sin describes the terrible labor experience Satan underwent. Satan was struck by a sudden, surprising, and dizzy pain as the angels stood praising God. In the light of the presence of God and the angels of heaven, Satan was in his own "darkness" (754). The left side of his head opened and from it was thrown something like him in shape and countenance (756). Satan produced a child in his own image, his own likeness, a parody of human birth. Adam "fathered in his likeness, according to his image" (Gen 5:3),6 and Satan fathered in his shape and countenance. This offspring of Satan plainly says to him, "Out of thy head I sprung" (758). Sin was birthed from the Devil.

Milton explains the origin of Sin clearly and purposefully. He uses Scriptural language to describe the birth of Sin from the head of Satan. Sin says she was birthed "Likest to thee in shape and count'nance bright" (756), which alludes to the manner of human birth (Gen 5:3). However, that text itself alludes to the creation of man in Genesis 1:26, where God says, "Let us make man in our image, according to our likeness." The Lord created man in his image, according to his likeness, and he alone was responsible for his creation, as he formed "the man of dust from the ground" (Gen 2:7) and "built the rib (which he had taken from the man) into a woman" (Gen 2:22). Moses summarizes man's creation, writing, "On the day God created him, he made him in the likeness of God" (Gen 5:1). In the same way, Sin was birthed from Satan, and as a direct result, Sin was made like him in "shape and count'nance" (756). Through the words of Sin, Milton is showing the creator, or originator, of evil. Sin is not said to be made in the image of God like man and woman. God, according to Milton, is not the author of sin. Rather, as a man fathers a child in his image and likeness (Gen 5:3), Satan fathers Sin in his shape and countenance. The appearance of Sin in the image and countenance of Satan is part of Milton's theodicy, as he uses it to demonstrate that Sin originates in the Devil, not in God.

After explaining her origin, which has significant implications for the origin of evil, Sin next relates the origin of her shadowy companion, Death. Following her birth from the head of Satan, Sin was met with mixed reactions by the angelic beings of heaven. Initially, "amazement seis'd All th' Host of Heav'n back they recoild afraid" (758–59). The angels were shocked at the spontaneous birth of Sin. However, this shock soon turned to rapture for some. Sin says to Satan,

familiar grown,
I pleas'd, and with attractive graces won
The most averse, thee chiefly, who full oft
Thy self in me thy perfect image viewing
Becam'st enamour'd. (761–65)

Sin did to Satan and "the most averse" of the angels what she still does today: she seduced them. Her "attractive graces" won them over to her. Her birth from the side of the Devil's head caused her to be born in his shape and countenance (756) and as a result, he saw the "perfect image" (764) of himself in her. In a clear reference to Narcissus, Satan loves the image of himself in Sin and becomes enamored with her. He lived in a perfect heaven, seeing and being in the presence of God. Satan ought to have remained enamored with God himself, but instead turns to his own image. Rather than being satisfied in God as the most blessed being, Satan seeks satisfaction in himself and falls in love with Sin.

Satan's love of his own image in Sin leads to their marital union. His secret intimacy with Sin (765–66) resulted in her conceiving a "growing burden" (766–67). Satan sinned with Sin and his sin produces a deadly consequence. Sin then briefly recounts the battle which Raphael relates in depth in Book VI, between the holy angels and the fallen angels. After the casting of Satan and the demons from heaven, Sin says she was given the Key to the Gates of Hell (774–75). Satan went into the inner parts of Hell with his demons, and Sin sat, keeping the gate. Her pregnancy progressed, and she describes her painful labor to Satan, saying, "At last this odious offspring whom thou seest Thine own begotten, breaking violent way Tore through my entrails" (781–83). She again emphasizes Satan as the source of this offspring with the phrase, "Thine own begotten" which along with her opening words to Death and Satan (727–28), is another parody of the Godhead. The child of Sin began to attack her immediately after his birth, so she named him Death (787). She then concludes their origin stories by telling of Death's rape of herself, which produced the "Hell Hounds" (654) coming out of her stomach. The birth of Death completes this sickening threesome. Milton's explanation of the origin of evil is not ambiguous. Satan, the father of the trio, conceived Sin in his own head, and their union produced Death. Sin and Death do not come from God, but are birthed from Satan.

The encounter between Satan and his offspring in Book II of Paradise Lost gives insight into the characters and serves as the major introduction for the reader to Milton's theology of evil. Surveying Sin's explanation to Satan of the origins of herself and Death lays the foundation for Milton's justification of God. Sin was made in the image and likeness of the Devil, not God. Death is the offspring of Sin and Satan, and not a part of God's original creation. The births of Sin and Death as reflected in Sin's discourse illustrate James 1:15, "Then when lust has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and when sin is accomplished, it brings forth death." Milton's presentation of Satan, Sin, and Death reflects this text perfectly. "Lust" connects to Satan, the father of both Sin and Death. Calvin, with whom the Reformed Puritan Milton would have been deeply familiar, said lust in James 1:15 functions as "the fountain of all evil affections."7 The Devil, in Book II, is the fountain of lust from which Sin is birthed. The conception and birth of sin "brings forth death" (Ja 1:15), just as the relations between Satan and Sin produced their shadowy child, Death.

The discourse between Satan and Sin not only illustrates James 1:15 but also connects to the larger context of the chapter. In James 1:13–17, God is presented as free from the stain of evil. When man is tempted to sin, he cannot blame God, because "God cannot be tempted by evil" (1:13). Rather, a man is tempted when he is "carried away and enticed by his own lust" (1:14). Lust gives birth to sin and death (1:15). Thus, the evil things of this world: lust, sin, death, are not from God, for "every good thing" is from him (1:17). James presents a theodicy, a justification of God in these five verses, that is in line with Milton's aim to "justifie the wayes of God to men" (1.26). James vindicates God by showing the source of temptation (lust, not God), the result of lust (sin and death), and the source of all good (God). Sin vindicates God in Book II, by showing Satan as the origin of herself and Death. The demonic duo was birthed, Sin from the head of the Devil, and Death from the relations of the demonic duo. Milton's theodicy includes the connection of Sin to Satan, but it also delves into the origin of Sin. Sin being birthed from the head of the Devil makes the Devil the cause of evil. Sin originates from Satan, and begets his child, Death. God, from whom every good thing comes (Ja 1:17), cannot be the cause of Sin in Milton's epic. Milton's graphic presentation of Sin's birth attributes her genesis to the Devil, and only the Devil. His vindication of God in this manner is drawn directly from an Augustinian doctrine of sin. God is not the efficient cause of evil, rather, the Devil begets it. Milton architects the birth of Sin to demonstrate Augustine's understanding of evil. God is justified by the will of the Devil.

The Augustinian Doctrine of Creation

Augustine, like many Christians, wrestled with the existence of evil. In his work Confessions, Augustine unpacks his theological journey to the God of the Bible and arrives at a key problem. Scripture boldly declares that God is the creator of all things (Gen 1:1, 31; Ex 20:11; Ps 33:6–9; Jn 1:3), and that God himself is good (Ex 34:6; 1 Chr 16:34; Mk 10:18; Ja 1:17). If God is good, and he created all things, then why is there evil in the world? Augustine reached a point in his thinking where he declared: "Although I affirmed and firmly held divine immunity... and the complete immutability of our God, the true God who made...all rational beings and everything, yet I had no clear and explicit grasp of the cause of evil."8 Scripture presents God as immune and immutable—guiltless and unchanging. Creation reflected God's goodness. As "all things came into being" (Jn 1:3)9 through the God who is alone good (Mk 10:18), so all things were made "very good" (Gen 1:31). Although creation images God's goodness, it does not reflect his immutability, as it is subject to corruption (Rom 8:20). Postlapsarian humans live in a world originally created very good by the immune and immutable God but that has fallen from its original state. Understanding evil requires following Augustine back to the creation of the world and the subsequent origin of evil.

Augustine readily affirms God's creation of all things from nothing.10 He writes that heaven and earth "cry aloud that they have not made themselves: 'the manner of our existence shows that we are made. For before we came to be, we did not exist to be able to make ourselves.'"11 Creation is not "possessed of being" like God; it does not have being of itself but derives its being. God is "I AM" (Ex 3:14), the self-existent one.12 Augustine references Exodus 3:14 and says it "implies that in comparison with him who really is, because he is unchangeable, the things created changeable have no real existence."13 God is unchangeable (immutable) because he is the one "who really is," he is pure being itself. However, his creation, because it is created is changeable. Creation does not have real existence, but derived existence. Augustine simply says to God, "They are because they come from you."14 Creation exists because it comes from the one who always exists. Anselm, a medieval theologian entrenched in Augustinian thought, declared, "Neither the Creator nor what he created can have their being from any source other than God himself."15 God has his being from himself, as he is self-existent, and the creature has its being from God, as it is not self-existent. Davison synthesizes Exodus 3, Augustine, and Anselm, writing, "Creatures, ultimately, exist only because God has created them: their being comes from God; they exist by participation in God's plenitude of being."16 Existence for the creature only comes when it participates in God's existence and derives existence from him. There is nothing that did not derive its becoming or being from God, the self-existent being.17

Since all things have their being from God, all things must also derive their goodness from God. In keeping with Genesis 1:31, Augustine describes heaven and earth (i.e., all creation) as beautiful and good. However, just as creation does not derive its being from itself, so also its beauty and goodness are not self-derived. Augustine refers to heaven and earth when he says to God, "they are not beautiful or good or possessed of being in the sense that you, their Maker are."18 God is the highest good and is only capable of doing good, so his creation was created good. Creation was not good because of itself, rather, it was good because God made it. Scott Christensen rightly states, "Goodness was necessarily present in all things at the moment of creation."19 Since all things that exist are good, Augustine concludes that existence itself is good.20 God made all things, so, "everything which in some degree has existence is good; for it derives from him who does not exist merely in some degree since he is Existence."21 The self-existent one created all, so all things derive existence from him. The self-existent one is pure good, so all things deriving existence from him, are created good. Existence is good because it is participation in the existence of God. Thomas Aquinas, following Augustine, logically concludes, "everything that has an essence is, by virtue of that fact, a good thing."22 All things with an essence come from God, for all existence is derived, except God's existence. All things that come from God are made good. Therefore, all things that exist, in their original state, must be good.

A Synthesis of Milton and Augustine on Creation

In Paradise Lost, Milton concurs with Augustine that since God is creator, all things have their existence from him and were created good. This may be demonstrated from one portion of Raphael's speech to Adam and Eve in Book V. Adam questions Raphael concerning the origin of all things, and the angel opens with these lines:

O Adam, one Almightie is, from whom
All things proceed, and up to him return,
If not deprav'd from good, created all
Such to perfection, one first matter all,
Indu'd with various forms, various degrees
Of substance, and in things that live, of life; (5.469–74)

The one Almighty is the source of all things and all things return to him. God is both the cause and goal of creation. All things must return to God because he is the fountain from which they came. Raphael's opening lines connect to Paul's doxology, "For from [ἐξ]23 him and through [δι'] him and to [εἰς] him are all things, to him be the glory to the ages, amen" (Rom 11:36). All things, "If not deprav'd from good" (471), will return to God, and "the creature's fundamental orientation is towards God."24 God created all creatures with the goal of "perfection" (472), and that perfection is realized in their return to him (470). Raphael then describes the metaphysics of creation: God made "one first matter" (472),25 and he endued that matter with forms, degrees of substance, and life (473–74). Milton uses Platonic and Augustinian concepts of creation in his reference to Plato's eternal forms (473). Augustine appropriated Platonic forms in his theology and referred to them as divine ideas in the mind of God.26 God gives all creatures their forms, substance, and life. Raphael's lines (473–74) parallel Augustine, as he says, "everything that exists—that is, whatever is contained just as it is in its own genus by its own nature—was produced by God as its maker; and that, with him as their maker, all living things are alive."27 For Augustine, everything that exists derives its substance, its genus (or form), and its life, from God alone. For Milton, everything that exists derives its form, substance, and life, from God alone. Milton's doctrine of creation and existence is thoroughly Augustinian.

Augustine's Doctrine of the Privatio Boni

Having established God as supremely good and the source of all things, Milton and Augustine must answer the question of the origin of evil. God is said to be the cause of all creation, and yet the good God cannot be the cause of evil. God is the cause of all that exists. Augustine understands that everything with being comes from God and is created good. However, although God created all things good, he did not create them immutable. All that God created was good, and it was all liable to corruption. Augustine writes, "It was obvious to me that things which are liable to corruption are good... For if they were supreme goods, they would be incorruptible."28 God is the supreme good, and as his creation is less than him, although it is created good, it is not the supreme good, and therefore corruptible.29 On the other hand, if the "things liable to corruption" were not good, "there would be nothing capable of being corrupted, for they are already not good."30 All beings are good, because they come from God, and as good, they are corruptible, for only a good thing can be corrupted. What does it mean to be corrupted? According to Augustine, "all things that are corrupted suffer privation of some good."31 Corruption is a privation, it is depriving a substance of its goodness. Augustine goes on to argue from these points that no substance is wholly given over to evil, for its very existence shows that there must be at least some good in it. Rather, evil things are things whose good has been corrupted to some extent. Aquinas concurs, saying succinctly that "No essence is evil in itself."32 Substances, according to Augustine, "if they are deprived of all good, they will be nothing at all. Therefore, as long as they exist, they are good."33 Existence, as established earlier, is good, so substances are therefore good. A substance utterly deprived, entirely evil, could not exist. Augustine then declares that "evil... is not a substance, for if it were a substance, it would be good."34 Herein lies the first key to Augustine's theodicy. Existence is good and corruption simply deprives a substance of good, so evil itself is not a substance.

If evil is not a substance, then what is it in Augustine's theology? He defines it outright: "Evil is not a positive substance: the loss of good has been given the name of 'evil'."35 Evil is the loss of good through privation. Privation, as Aquinas notes, "is not an essence, it is, rather, a negation in a substance. Therefore, evil is not an essence in things."36 This definition of evil is named the privatio boni,37 the privation of good. Evil itself does not have being, because if it did, it would have been created by God, who is goodness itself. All substances derive being and goodness from God. Evil, however, is not a substance, and it is not good, but a privation of goodness. The goodness that evil privates is God's goodness as reflected in the creature, so evil is actually "a deprivation of the supreme goodness and beauty of God himself."38 Evil is the negation of good that God created, indeed, a damper upon creation's reflection of God's goodness. God could not have created evil as it is not a substance at all, but a privation of God's good creation. Thus, God is not the cause of evil. Instead, Augustine finds the cause of evil, at least a partial one, in the will.

To understand the origin of evil, Augustine turns to its first occurrence: the fall of the Devil and his angels. The fall of the evil angels, and the subsequent fall of man and woman, do not stem from any defect within their creation. Augustine repeatedly establishes all substances as created good by the God from whom they have their being. He thus says the difference between good and evil angels "did not arise from any difference in nature or origin... since God created both, and he is good in his creation and fashioning of all substances."39 There was nothing inherent in the substance of angels that determined whether they would be good or evil. The privation of good in a substance cannot come from the substance, for then evil would originate from good. Returning to Aquinas, "Evil cannot be natural to any subject, since it is a privation of what is natural."40 Evil is the privation of good, so good must pre-exist evil. Good is natural, it is how God created all things, and evil is the unnatural privation of good. Evil is not created by God and was not present in the original creation. Evil originates at the level of the creature, not the creator. As a privation, rather than an essence, evil does not come from the created substance of a creature. Augustine thus concludes that the difference between good and evil angels "had its origin in their wills and desires."41 Evil arises from the will. Evil cannot come from God, so it cannot come from the substance of the creature. The will is the birthplace of evil.

The fall of the Devil, the first evil, came from a perversion of his will. God is the highest good, and as the highest good he made all creatures to find their purpose, their goal, and their highest happiness, in him. Creatures have their being from God, and it is through participation in him that they exist as he created them to. Augustine contrasts the good angels with the evil angels, saying that the good angels fixed their wills on God, "for the bliss of the good angels is their adherence to him who supremely is."42 The good angels adhered to God, the one who is, the self-existent one. Milton demonstrates this in Book III of Paradise Lost, as he says that the angels saw the Father and "from his sight receiv'd Beatitude past utterance" (3.61–62). Milton concurred with Augustine that "there is only one Good which will bring happiness to a rational or intellectual creature; and that Good is God."43 The supreme good made his good creatures to find their aim in him (Rom 11:36). The good angels, then, held fast to God as their creator and the highest good.

In contrast with the good angels, the evil angels turned from God. Evil is birthed from the will, and it comes from "turning away from him who supremely is."44 The angels who would fall turned their wills away from God, and in so doing, turned from goodness itself. The cause of evil cannot be found in the creation of the angels, or man. The angels were made good, and yet corruptible, for good creation is still corruptible. The possibility for corruption lay in the freedom of the will. Angels, and man, were created with a will, with the possibility of choice. In keeping with Augustine, Anselm wrote, "The fallen angel and the first human being sinned through free choice, since they sinned through their own choice, which was so free that it could not be compelled to sin by any other thing."45 The will of the Devil and man was uncompelled, and they freely chose to turn from God, their highest good.46 Augustine says that the evil angels "have become evil by their own bad will; and this bad will did not originate from their nature which was good. It came through a voluntary falling away from the good, so that evil is caused not by good, but by falling away from good."47 Good nature cannot cause an evil will. Rather, the will fell away from God as its highest good. This falling away cannot be tied back to their good nature, but simply to the freedom of their choice.48 Evil is birthed from the will, and it comes from a will that has rejected God for something lesser. Evil is not a created substance, but a privation of good, springing from the will that turns away from God as its source of good.

Augustine's understanding of evil may be summarized with this sentence: "I inquired what wickedness is, and I did not find a substance but a perversity of will twisted away from the highest substance, you O God, toward inferior things, rejecting its own inner life... and swelling with external matter."49 Evil is a lack of substance, it consists in the will turning away from God, and so depriving the being of the highest good. Evil is a rejection of God, for something lesser, and its sole birth is in the will. As the source of becoming and being for all essences, God makes them good in their original state. The original creation did not include wickedness. So evil is not a substance, but a privation of the good that God has created. The origin of evil cannot be found in creation, or in the substance of a creature itself, for good cannot be the formal cause of evil. The will must be the origin of evil, for the privation of good occurs when the will turns away from the good God who made it. God is not the father of evil. Evil is not created but birthed, and its mother is the will. This is the theodicy of Augustine, and it is likewise the theodicy of John Milton.

Milton's Depiction of the Privatio Boni

Sin's retelling of her origin in Book II of Paradise Lost not only shows who is to be blamed for evil, but also shows its direct cause. As God created man in his image and Adam fathered a son in his image and likeness, the Devil fathered Sin in his own countenance and shape, such that she could call herself his "perfect image" (2.764). However, the imagery goes deeper and shows not only the father of Sin (Satan) but also her mother. Sin's description of her birth bears repeating, as it locates her precise birthplace:

All on a sudden miserable pain
Surprisd thee, dim thine eyes, and dizzie swumm
In darkness, while thy head flames thick and fast
Threw forth, till on the left side op'ning wide,
Likest to thee in shape and count'nance bright,
Then shining Heav'nly fair, a Goddess arm'd
Out of thy head I sprung; (752–58)

As noted earlier, Sin describes Satan's labor process in her birth. Sin is birthed from a specific part of Satan. His head becomes dizzy and is thrown into darkness; then the left side of his head opened wide (755) and Sin sprung out of it (758). Sin was born out of the head of Satan. It was Satan's own mind, will, and desires that brought Sin into existence. Sin's description of her birth is directly in line with Augustine. Evil, for Augustine, finds its place of origin in the turning of the will away from God. Sin, for Satan, finds her origin in his head—the location of his will. In the birth of Sin from Satan's head, one can see the "perversity of will twisted away from the highest substance."50 Satan turned away from God as the highest substance, and out of the perversity of his will, Sin was born. Augustine found the source of the fall of Satan in his "wills and desires,"51 and this is precisely what Milton portrays. Sin does not come from God, she does not come from external temptation, rather, she comes directly from the head of Satan. Sin, in Paradise Lost, has the Devil for a father, and his will for a mother.

Milton presents God as the creator and sustainer of all substances (5.469–74), but Evil, in Augustinian thought, is not a substance. Rather, evil is the privation of good. Therefore, Milton does not present God as the cause of evil, but the will of the Devil as its birthplace. Raphael describes the initial revolt of Satan in Book V, and it matches Sin's earlier recounting in Book II. After the Father presented the Son to all the angels of heaven, they responded with feasts and praises. Yet there was one angel who did not rejoice. He was "fraught With envie against the Son of God, that day" (661–62). The Son of God was honored by his Father and appointed as the Messiah (663–64), and this was a sight the angel "could not beare" (664). The angel ought to have responded to the Son of God, the highest good, the self-existent one, with praise and delight. Instead, he turned his will away from God and turned to himself in envy (662). The angel desired to have the place of honor occupied by God's Son. Augustine, in reference to this turning of the will, asks, "What other name is there for this fault than pride?"52 Milton concurs with Augustine, stating that the angel "could not beare" (664) the sight of God because of his "pride" (665). Through his pride, the angel could not bear to look upon the Son, and so he turned away from God with envy. It was at this point that the angel fell. Milton begins his next sentence with the words, "Deep malice thence conceiving and disdain" (666).53 The conception of Sin occurred as Satan turned away from the Son in envy. Sin was conceived as the "perversity of will twisted away from the highest substance."54 In Augustinian thought, sin is conceived from the will as it turns away from God in pride. In Milton's presentation of Satan and Sin, the angel conceives sin in his head (will) as he turns away from God in pride. Sin for Milton is not a substance created by God, but the privatio boni, birthed from the will of the Devil. Milton follows Augustine's understanding of Satan, his will, and the origin of evil. His descriptions and portrayal of the characters of Satan, Sin, and Death are designed to exhibit Augustine's theology. Sin is birthed, not from God, but from Satan as her father, and his will as her mother.

Conclusion

The conception of evil as the privatio boni and the will of the Devil as its source is a sufficient justification of God in Miltonic and Augustinian thought. Yet there is more to the doctrine of evil than its birth. The biblical God, and Milton's presentation of him, does not stand back from this world of evil, but brings good out of it. The theodicy that Scripture and Paradise Lost present not only withholds blame from God but also highlights his work in redemptive history to defeat evil. The story of evil may begin with its birth in the will of the Devil, but it ends with the defeat of all wickedness. The holy God ends every privation of good through two primary means: the restoration of Eden and the sacrifice of Christ.

In Book X, after judgment is pronounced on Satan, Adam, and Eve, and the devils are turned into serpents, Sin and Death discuss their evil plan. The two children of Satan intend to use the fall as an opening to wreak havoc on the now-cursed creation. Sin calls the world their "Empire" (10.592) and presents it to Death as a bounty of food to satisfy his never-ending hunger (591–95, 603–09). She says that she will reside in Man (607), and "season him" to be Death's "last and sweetest prey" (609). Through the fall of man and woman, Sin will infect each of their children and bring them to an end through Death's feeding. The privatio boni appears to win and destroy God's creation, through the work of Sin and Death. But the Father hears the words of Sin and Death and responds to the contrary. He acknowledges that the "Dogs of Hell advance to waste and havoc yonder World" (616–17), the very world that he created "fair and good" (617). Yet it is through their actions that God will restore his creation. He says Sin and Death think they are fighting against him, but he is using them as his "Hell-hounds, to lick up the draff and filth Which mans polluting Sin with taint hath shed On what was pure" (630–33). God will extinguish evil from the world through the Death that Sin brings. God is not only blameless of guilt from the existence of Sin and Death, but he uses them for a positive end to eradicate all privations of good.

After using Sin and Death to remove the effects of the fall, God will destroy Satan's children themselves. There will come a time when Death will have eaten its fill of the fall's pollution (631–33). At that point, the Father says,

at one sling
Of thy victorious Arm, well-pleasing Son,
Both Sin, and Death, and yawning Grave at last
Through Chaos hurld, obstruct the mouth of Hell
For ever, and seal up his ravenous Jawes (633–37)

Sin and Death will be defeated by the arm of God's Son. God will be victorious over the children of Satan. When Sin and Death have been destroyed, "Then Heav'n and Earth renewd shall be made pure To sanctitie that shall receive no staine" (638–39). Evil, the privatio boni, will be eradicated forever as Sin and Death are thrown into hell. Good will reign, and it will never again be deprived. The sanctified creation "shall receive no staine" (639) and will continue in pure goodness forever. God's goodness will be fully revealed in creation, and it will never come to an end. The new creation will be incorruptible. The fall of Satan, and the fall of Adam and Eve, will be completely reversed. The defeat of Sin and Death is foretold in Book X, but what of the Devil himself? Michael the Archangel tells of Satan's defeat in Book XII, and he says that Satan will also be defeated by the Son. Although Satan has sought to wage war against the Son in combat, he will be overcome by the obedience of the Son. In his "reproachful life and cursed death" (12.406), the Son stands in the place of Adam, keeping the law he broke and paying the penalty for his disobedience. The life, death, and resurrection of the Son:

Shall bruise the head of Satan, crush his strength
Defeating Sin and Death, his two maine armes,
And fix farr deeper in his head thir stings
Then temporal death shall bruise the Victors heel (430–33)

The defeat of Satan will come by the Son of God crushing his head, in fulfillment of Genesis 3:15. His armies, Sin and Death, will also lose their power through the crushing of his head, and the evil threesome will be defeated by the God who is three in one. Satan, Sin, and Death are no match for the Father, Son, and Spirit.

Milton brings his theodicy to its fullness in Books X and XII, as God is not only guiltless of evil but also brings about its ultimate defeat. Milton follows Augustine's teaching that God is not the cause of evil, for evil is a privation of the goodness he created. Instead, evil is birthed from a will that has defected from God as its highest good. Milton demonstrates these truths through the birth of Sin from the will of Satan. Satan is the father of Sin, and his will is her mother. The God who is innocent of evil will ultimately restore the paradise that was lost, and he will do so through his Son. Sin and Death, when they have eaten their fill, will be done away with forever, and the creation that suffered under them will be restored. The Devil will be defeated by the Second Adam, the seed of the woman he first deceived. Satan's defeat will come via the crushing of his head, the very place from which he conceived Sin. Evil will be done away with and the head which birthed it will be crushed.
