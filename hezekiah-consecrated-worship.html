Hezekiah's Attempted Restoration Through Consecrated Worship

Zachary Urdaneta · April 15, 2022

Hezekiah, a king of Judah during the divided kingdom of Israel, ascended the throne in a time of spiritual rebellion[cite: 5]. Unlike the nation, he is presented in an overall positive light (2 Chr 29:2) in the three passages of the Old Testament focused on him: 2 Kings 18–20, 2 Chronicles 29–32, and Isaiah 36–39[cite: 5]. His father Ahaz was a wicked ruler who forsook fidelity to the Lord (Isa 7), yet Hezekiah did not follow in the sins of his father[cite: 5]. At the outset of his reign, he initiated comprehensive religious reforms (2 Chr 29–31; 2 Kgs 18:1–6)[cite: 5]. These reforms only served the nation of Judah, as the estranged people of Israel continued in their sin[cite: 5]. During his coregency with Ahaz, Hezekiah witnessed the siege of Samaria and the ensuing exile of the Northern Kingdom (2 Kgs 18:9–12)[cite: 5]. Contrastingly Judah was not carried into exile during his reign because they trusted in the Lord under their faithful king[cite: 5]. From these accounts, Hezekiah appears to be a faithful and righteous king, portrayed as the potential Davidic descendant who will reign righteously (2 Sam 7:12–13; Isa 9:2–7)[cite: 5]. However, the record presents additional information about the reign of Hezekiah—including his openness to Babylon (Isa 39; 2 Kgs 20) and his tribute to Assyria (2 Kgs 18:14–16)—that shows he did not always demonstrate perfect trust in God[cite: 5]. An analysis of Hezekiah's life, particularly his early religious reforms presented in 2 Chronicles 29 and 30, instructs the reader concerning Hezekiah, repentance, and the character of the promised righteous king[cite: 5]. In anticipation of the Messiah, Hezekiah led Judah in consecrated worship and attempted a failed restoration of the remnant through his epistle's call to repentance[cite: 5].

Although there are issues related to the chronology of Hezekiah's life, it is clear that 2 Kings 18:1–12 and 2 Chronicles 29–31 describe his actions at the beginning of his reign.1 These chapters relate the religious reforms that Hezekiah undertook after becoming sole ruler over Judah[cite: 5]. Second Kings summarizes his reforms briefly, while 2 Chronicles focuses on their relation to consecrated temple worship[cite: 5]. The overall theme portrayed in Chronicles is the right worship of God, and 1 Chronicles devotes extensive detail to the construction of the temple, the location of proper worship[cite: 5]. Chronicles focuses on the kings of the nation of Judah during the divided kingdom, especially the theological implications of their lives and their relationship to temple worship[cite: 5]. Second Chronicles then is an ideal basis for studying Hezekiah's religious reformation[cite: 5].

The Chronicler states that Hezekiah "became king when he was twenty-five years old" (2 Chr 29:1), in 715[cite: 5]. According to Merrill, this is the beginning of his sole rule over Judah, as he "became coregent with Ahaz in the third year of Hoshea of Israel (2 Kings 18:1), 729 BC" when he was eleven years old.2 Hezekiah's reign begins with him opening and repairing the doors of the temple (2 Chr 29:3)[cite: 5]. This reopening was necessary as his father Ahaz had "closed the doors of the house of the LORD and made altars for himself in every corner of Jerusalem" (2 Chr 28:24)[cite: 5]. These actions by Ahaz were a blatant rebellion against the Lord[cite: 5]. The king did not have authority over the temple as that was designated to the priests and Levites (Num 25:13; Deut 18:1)[cite: 5]. Ahaz overstepped his authority to lead the people astray from the Lord[cite: 5]. Even if the altars he made were to worship the Lord, they would still not be legitimate[cite: 5]. God demands to be worshipped in the way that he commands[cite: 5]. As Ahaz's actions were in direct defiance of the Lord, Hezekiah's first actions are in direct defiance of his father[cite: 5]. He previously reigned with Ahaz for fourteen years as coregent, and his father's "apostasy, without question, made a profound impact on the young prince himself."3 Hezekiah's initial opening of the temple made a strong statement that he would reign in obedience to the Lord[cite: 5]. The literal action of unbarring the temple doors also describes the reforms of Hezekiah metaphorically: he is opening the door to true and faithful worship of the Lord[cite: 5].

Merely opening the doors of the temple was not enough to restore the people to true worship of the Lord[cite: 5]. God demands holy worship[cite: 5]. Ahaz had defiled the temple, so Hezekiah called the Levites to consecrate themselves and afterwards consecrate the temple itself in order to "carry the uncleanness out from the holy place" (2 Chr 29:5)[cite: 5]. Hezekiah is the one who summons them (2 Chr 29:4) and instructs them in the importance of consecrated worship (2 Chr 29:6–11)[cite: 5]. This may seem strange as "one would naturally expect the task of purifying the Temple to fall to the priests or the clergy in general" but here "both the initiative and responsibility remain with the king."4 Despite these unexpected events, the Chronicler gives no indication that Hezekiah is at fault for his near-priestly work[cite: 5]. He is not circumventing the prescribed order, unlike his father Ahaz[cite: 5]. Rather he is taking necessary steps to lead the people to return to the Lord[cite: 5]. His motives are made clear by his speech to the priests and Levites in 2 Chronicles 29:6–11[cite: 5].

Hezekiah begins his speech by recognizing the sins of their fathers, stating they "have been unfaithful and have done evil in the sight of the LORD our God, and have forsaken Him and turned their faces away from the dwelling place of the LORD, and have turned their backs" (2 Chr 29:6)[cite: 5]. This harsh language shows the severity of the sins of Judah[cite: 5]. They followed the Lord at times but had utterly abandoned him leading up to Hezekiah's sole rule[cite: 5]. The nation's turning away was made visible by the closure of the temple under Ahaz so that the people quite literally "turned their faces away from the dwelling place of the LORD"[cite: 5]. The physical turning from the temple coincided with a spiritual turning from the Lord[cite: 5]. Hezekiah expands this concept in the following verse, showing the closure of the temple meant incense and sacrifices were not offered to the Lord (29:7)[cite: 5]. As the people turned from the temple, they also turned from the true worship of the Lord[cite: 5]. The language of turning here is also used throughout the Old Testament to describe turning back to the Lord in repentance[cite: 5]. Therefore, this anti-repentance must be rectified by true repentance[cite: 5]. As the closure and defilement of the temple caused the people to turn from the Lord, so the opening and consecration of the temple must be the first step in leading the people to return to the Lord[cite: 5].

Hezekiah recognizes the reason for God's judgment upon Judah (and upon the exiled Northern Kingdom) is their lack of true worship (2 Chr 29:8–9)[cite: 5]. The Lord's outstretched anger against them will not be removed unless they repent and worship him faithfully[cite: 5]. Hezekiah thus says in verse 10, "Now it is in my heart to make a covenant with the LORD God of Israel, that His burning anger may turn away from us"[cite: 5]. The nature of this covenant is not apparent within the text[cite: 5]. Merrill argues Hezekiah is attempting to renew the Mosaic covenant, however, the Hebrew phrase used by Hezekiah, לִכְרוֹת בְּרִית (likrot berit, "to cut a covenant") is largely used in the Old Testament to refer to making a new covenant or agreement.5 Others have raised the possibility that this is an oath before God, but regardless of what the covenant entailed, the purpose of Hezekiah's statement remains: the people must wholeheartedly devote themselves to the Lord with the same devotion as someone cutting a covenant.6 He likewise urges the priests to "not be negligent," but to serve the Lord in their ministry of consecrated worship (29:11)[cite: 5]. This consecration "was the first step in restoring fellowship with God...since only a true priesthood could offer acceptable worship."7 Hezekiah recognized that acceptable worship is necessary for fellowship with a holy God[cite: 5].

The spiritual leadership of Hezekiah is echoed by the Levites who in turn lead their fellow priests[cite: 5]. The Levitical leaders, named in 29:12–14, "assembled their brothers, consecrated themselves, and went in to cleanse the house of the LORD" (29:15)[cite: 5]. The newly consecrated priests follow the leadership of their king by cleansing and consecrating the temple[cite: 5]. A righteous king shepherds his people to likewise walk in righteousness[cite: 5]. The action of cleansing (29:15, 16) denotes "the deliverance of the Temple from a state of 'pollution', the removal of an essentially negative condition."8 The cleansing of the temple from its pollution resulted in a state of consecration—the same state the Levites themselves were in (29:5, 15).9 The word qadaš, primarily meaning holy or sacred,10 is used throughout the Pentateuch in commands given to Israel[cite: 5]. The Lord instructed Moses to give this charge, calling the people to be "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:6)[cite: 5]. Their holiness was to be reflected in separation from the pagan nations and consecrated worship to the Lord[cite: 5]. Leviticus and Deuteronomy lay out in detail how this holy living and worship should look[cite: 5]. The purpose of Israel was to be a holy nation[cite: 5]. Thus, it was of the utmost importance for the priests and the temple to be consecrated so that holy worship may be offered to a holy God[cite: 5].

Hezekiah was acting as a righteous king, leading the necessary cleansing preparations for sanctified worship[cite: 5]. While only in the first month of his sole reign, his thoroughness in leading the people spiritually from the beginning was a clear indication of his priorities[cite: 5]. Matthew Henry notes, "He found Judah low and naked yet did not make it his first business to revive the civil interests of his kingdom, but to restore religion to its good posture again."11 Hezekiah's priority was the true worship of the Lord[cite: 5]. With the priests and the temple consecrated, he begins to lead worship again in 2 Chronicles 29:20–36[cite: 5]. He brings "the princes of the city" with him to the house of the Lord (2 Chr 29:20), showing that he once again takes an active role in the restoration of true worship[cite: 5]. Hezekiah does not overstep his role in leading worship as he commands "the sons of Aaron" (2 Chr 29:21) to offer burnt offerings[cite: 5]. The Chronicler details their day of worship, focusing on their sacrifices and singing[cite: 5].

Hezekiah, the princes, and the priests sacrificed seven bulls, rams, lambs, and male goats "for a sin offering for the kingdom, the sanctuary, and Judah" (2 Chr 29:21)[cite: 5]. The burnt offerings are the culmination of the consecration of the temple[cite: 5]. The goal is to offer sacrifices for the whole nation[cite: 5]. As seen before, the closing of the temple had led the people away from God in an anti-repentance[cite: 5]. With the temple reopen and newly consecrated, these sacrifices mark the first day of consecrated worship to the Lord[cite: 5]. In this worship, Hezekiah "seems to have partly modeled the occasion on the Day of Atonement, for that was the only day each year when every sin was sacrificially atoned for."12 The laying of hands on the goats (29:23) also recollects the role of the scapegoat on the same Day (Lev 16:20–22)[cite: 5]. The sacrifices offered to the Lord demonstrate that the hearts of the people present are truly repentant[cite: 5]. The Chronicler emphasizes twice in 29:24 that the sacrifices are offered "for all Israel"[cite: 5]. They are not merely for the people present, or Hezekiah's Southern Kingdom, "but Israelites in general (the twelve tribes), for whom the temple in Jerusalem was the only lawful sanctuary."13 The exile of Israel by Assyria left them without even their false places of worship[cite: 5]. Hezekiah recognized that the sin of the Northern Kingdom was the reason for their exile (2 Chr 30:6–9) and began the process of attempted restoration through an offering on account of it (2 Chr 29:21)[cite: 5]. North or South, Israel's need was repentance through a return to proper worship of the Lord[cite: 5].

The second aspect of this initial day of sacrificial worship is the singing[cite: 5]. Second Chronicles 29:25–30 describes the musical worship of the Levites as the animals were sacrificed[cite: 5]. The Lord had commanded this worship through David and Nathan the prophet (29:25) and the combination of sacrifice and singing was pleasing to him[cite: 5]. This combination "symbolizes...the harmony that ought to exist between the physical and spiritual aspects of worship."14 The Old Testament repeatedly testifies that sacrifice without earnestness of heart is meaningless[cite: 5]. In the words of Samuel, "has the LORD as much delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices as in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to heed than the fat of rams" (1 Sam 15:22; cf. Isa 1:10–17)[cite: 5]. The sincerity of their praise is characterized by humility and joy (2 Chr 29:30) and joy "is the conclusive expression of the fact that a deed has been executed with the whole heart."15 The joyous praise of the people of God is directly connected to atonement for sin[cite: 5]. In fact, "the praise of God is made possible only by the previous removal of sin and only a forgiven person can truly sing God's praises."16 In 2 Chronicles 29, Hezekiah has led a repentant people in offering atoning sacrifices as they sing praise to the Lord in his consecrated temple[cite: 5]. Ultimately God was the one who brought this repentance to pass (29:36), and he continues to act in the hearts of his people after "the service of the house of the LORD was established" (29:35)[cite: 5].

Following a successful consecration of the priests and the temple, and a reopening of consecrated worship to the Lord in Jerusalem, Hezekiah turns his focus to the entire people of Israel[cite: 5]. Having offered a sacrifice for the sake of both the North and South (2 Chr 29:24), he now attempts to personally include all Israel in worship[cite: 5]. He sends letters to all of Israel and Judah, which includes Ephraim and Manasseh (2 Chr 30:1), representative of the whole Northern Kingdom.17 Although the Northern tribes were largely carried off by Assyria (2 Kgs 18:9–12),18 there were still some who remained in the land (2 Chr 30:6)[cite: 5]. Hezekiah calls these Northern survivors to celebrate the Passover with the Southern Kingdom[cite: 5]. The Passover had obviously been neglected under the anti-repentance of King Ahaz as he shut down the temple[cite: 5]. A restoration of this feast is an essential part of the repentance of Hezekiah and his nation, as it later proved to be for Josiah, as well (2 Kgs 23, 2 Chr 34–35)[cite: 5].

The Chronicler emphasizes in 2 Chronicles 30:2–4 that Hezekiah's Passover was celebrated in the second month[cite: 5]. Two reasons are given: an insufficient number of consecrated priests, and the inability to gather all the people in Jerusalem (2 Chr 30:3)[cite: 5]. The celebration of Passover under Hezekiah in the second month was a special circumstance provided for in the law[cite: 5]. When Israel observed the Passover in their second year of wilderness wandering, some unclean men were unable to join the rest of the nation (Num 9:6)[cite: 5]. Concerning these men who failed to celebrate at the appointed time, the Lord declared they could celebrate in the second month on the same day (Num 9:10–11)[cite: 5]. The exception was not for a neglectful person (Num 9:13) but only for those on a distant journey or ceremonially unclean (Num 9:10)[cite: 5]. In the case of Hezekiah's people in 2 Chronicles 30, "[b]oth these reasons existed...the priests had not sufficiently sanctified themselves, and the people had not assembled in Jerusalem, at the legal time in the first month."19 Despite the date change, Hezekiah's Passover was still in accordance with the Lord's prescription for proper worship[cite: 5]. The motivation for calling the remnant of the North to join them was a recognition that "they had not celebrated as a multitude as it had been written" (2 Chr 30:5, author's translation)[cite: 5]. Everything about the consecration of chapter 29 and the Passover of chapter 30 was according to the written law of the Lord[cite: 5]. Obedience to God is necessary for true repentance and worship and an essential aspect to a "kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:5–6)[cite: 5].

Instead of moving to the celebration of the feast, the narrative relates Hezekiah's letter calling the Northern survivors to Jerusalem[cite: 5]. The proclamation from Hezekiah reveals his view of the Assyrian captivity and his theology of repentance[cite: 5]. The issue of exile and return has been central to the Jewish people ever since the first exile of the Northern Kingdom[cite: 5]. Much of Old Testament prophecy is concerned with the judgment of exile, a call for repentance, and the promised restoration of the repentant remnant[cite: 5]. Hezekiah's letter is essentially the earliest written piece on the exile of the Northern Kingdom and provides important insight into the event[cite: 5]. He writes to the remainder of the Northern tribes that were carried into exile by the Assyrians[cite: 5]. Their fathers were "stiff-necked" (2 Chr 30:8, ESV) so God's anger was turned toward them (29:6–9)[cite: 5]. The Lord's anger did not destroy the people entirely, but it did affect them forever[cite: 5]. Therefore, the people remaining are urged to return to the Lord in Hezekiah's epistle[cite: 5].

Hezekiah begins his letter with this call, "O sons of Israel, return to the LORD GOD of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, that He may return to those of you who escaped and are left from the hand of the kings of Assyria" (2 Chr 30:6)[cite: 5]. The word for Israel's return in Hezekiah's letter (2 Chr 30:6–9), šûb, covers both the physical and spiritual return[cite: 5]. A common word, šûb can simply mean to physically "move in an opposite direction."20 The Northern survivors must physically return to Jerusalem, yet in this case šûb has more than just a physical connotation[cite: 5]. The five occurrences of šûb in the letter of Hezekiah are in the qal stem, and "the various aspects of religious repentance and its opposite dominate the semantic categories of the qal in theological contexts."21 Theologically in Old Testament usage, šûb references both turning away from the Lord and turning back to him in anti-repentance and repentance[cite: 5]. All of the Old Testament "expressions of man's penitential activity...are subsumed and summarized by this one verb šûb. For better than any other verb it combines in itself the two requisites of repentance: to turn from evil and to turn to the good."22 Thus the word šûb allows for both physical and spiritual returning[cite: 5]. In 2 Chronicles 30, šûb refers to the physical return of the Northern Kingdom to the temple of Jerusalem, and their spiritual return (repentance) that results from the physical return[cite: 5]. The implication is, "when Israel returns to God in repentance...their exiles will physically return...to the Promised Land."23 A spiritual return to the Lord was the purpose behind Hezekiah's reopening of the temple in defiance of Ahaz's anti-repentance[cite: 5]. The consecrated temple must be filled with consecrated people worshipping the Lord[cite: 5]. The people of God, particularly the Northern Kingdom, must physically return to the temple as a key step in spiritually returning to the Lord[cite: 5].

In many of its occurrences, the spiritual turning that šûb describes can refer to a return from exile and "the covenant community's return to God."24 The return from exile to the Lord is the context of the repentance in Hezekiah's letter[cite: 5]. Additional aid in understanding the context of the letter is two other key words it contains: š'r (שאר) and pĕlêṭâ (פְּלֵיטָה)[cite: 5]. The š'r root is commonly used in the Old Testament to refer to a "remnant," while pĕlêṭâ essentially denotes escapees or survivors[cite: 5]. The New American Standard Bible translates these words as descriptions of the sons of Israel, saying God will return "to those of you who escaped [pĕlêṭâ] and are left [š'r]"[cite: 5]. Alternatively, the opening call of Hezekiah's letter could be translated, "Sons of Israel, return to the Lord God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, that he may return to the remnant of you who have escaped [pĕlêṭâ] from the hand of the kings of Assyria."25 The only Israelites of the Northern Kingdom remaining in their land are the pĕlêṭâ—the escapees[cite: 5]. The rightful name for them is the remnant[cite: 5]. The term remnant is widely used in the Old Testament26 to refer to survivors in captivity or those left in the land after an exile[cite: 5]. At the time of 2 Chronicles 29–30, the only survivors in the Northern Kingdom are those who escaped from the king of Assyria as he led most of the nation into exile[cite: 5].

Although Hezekiah recognizes the scarcity of the escaped remnant living in the land of Israel, he still holds out hope for their exiled brethren[cite: 5]. He makes a bold promise to the escaped remnant, writing, "if you return [šûb] to the LORD, your brothers and your sons will find compassion before those who led them captive and will return [šûb] to this land" (2 Chr 30:9)[cite: 5]. Once again, the physical and spiritual returns are connected[cite: 5]. If Israel physically returns to the temple as a spiritual return to the Lord, the captives will physically return to the land, and likewise spiritually return to the Lord[cite: 5]. The significance of this call for a return must not be missed[cite: 5]. The exiles of the Northern and Southern kingdoms of Israel were the most devastating events in the history of the nation[cite: 5]. Israel was to be "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:6) and the consecrated reopening of temple worship was a massive step toward that ideal[cite: 5]. Throughout its history, Israel was largely led by unrighteous leaders, and their times of obedience were stained, at best[cite: 5]. Even under the reign of the nation's greatest king, there was widespread civil war sparked by David's own son[cite: 5]. Israel never truly achieved its intended status as a holy nation, and with the exile of the Northern kingdom, their hope seemed all but lost[cite: 5]. Hezekiah's hope is for God to restore the captive exiles if their remnant in the land is repentant[cite: 5]. The hope of Hezekiah is grounded in prophetic history, particularly in the final chapters of Deuteronomy[cite: 5].

Through the prophet Moses, the Lord foretold both the exile of the nation and the restoration of the remnant[cite: 5]. As Israel prepared to enter the promised land, Moses gave his final messages expounding upon the law, which were recorded as the book of Deuteronomy[cite: 5]. Chapters 27–30 of Deuteronomy describe the blessings and curses upon the nation if they obey or disobey Moses' commands[cite: 5]. Chief among the promised punishments is the threat of exile[cite: 5]. Moses declares if they reject the law of the Lord, "[t]he LORD will bring you and your king, whom you set over you, to a nation which neither you nor your fathers have known, and there you shall serve other gods, wood and stone" (Deut 28:36)[cite: 5]. Any punishment God would bring upon them in their own land is exceeded by the punishment of being driven into another country as captives[cite: 5]. The punishment of exile would be just and deserved, as it would come upon them for their lack of obedience (Deut 28:45)[cite: 5]. The people had numerous opportunities to serve the Lord, but because they rejected him, they would instead serve their enemies (Deut 28:46–47)[cite: 5]. The judgment brought upon them by the Lord through a conquering nation would leave them "few in number" (Deut 28:62; cf. Isa 10:22)[cite: 5]. The descriptions of exile in Deuteronomy 28 perfectly describe the state of the Northern Kingdom in the time of Hezekiah[cite: 5].

Since Israel had been exiled as prophesied in Deuteronomy 28, they also had the hope of restoration promised to the same people in Deuteronomy 30[cite: 5]. Moses says to the people that if they remember these words in the land of their exile, "and you return [šûb] to the LORD your God and obey Him with all your heart and soul...then the LORD your God will restore [šûb] you from captivity" (Deut 30:2–3)[cite: 5]. These prophecies of return and restoration are certain, as "the grammatical pattern suggests a lack of any true conditionality here. When the exiles come to pass, so would these acts of repentance and restoration."27 The return of the exiles will occur, no matter if the "outcasts are at the ends of the earth" (Deut 30:4)[cite: 5]. Isaiah describes the same event, prophesying that God will restore "the remnant of His people" from "the four corners of the earth" (Isa 11:11–12)[cite: 5]. Hezekiah focused on both the physical return of the captives and the spiritual return of all the nation, and Moses does the same[cite: 5]. He prophesies that God will bring the people into their land again (Deut 30:5) and "circumcise your heart and the heart of your descendants, to love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul, so that you may live" (Deut 30:6)[cite: 5]. The heart circumcision is the spiritual return[cite: 5]. In Deuteronomy 28–30, Moses is in part speaking of the people of Hezekiah's day[cite: 5]. Hezekiah is following the pattern of return and restoration laid out by Moses, calling for a physical and spiritual return[cite: 5].

Hezekiah's glorious epistle calling for a physical and spiritual return is sent "throughout all Israel and Judah" (2 Chr 30:6)[cite: 5]. However, the reaction to the letter carriers was not what he intended, as men of the Northern Kingdom "laughed them to scorn and mocked them" (2 Chr 30:10)[cite: 5]. The response was not entirely negative, though, for some men from Israel "humbled themselves and came to Jerusalem" (2 Chr 30:11)[cite: 5]. In contrast to the mixed response of the remnant, "the hand of God was also on Judah to give them one heart to do what the king and the princes commanded by the word of the LORD" (2 Chr 30:12)[cite: 5]. The Chronicler makes the contrast clear between the repentant and unrepentant[cite: 5]. Those who came to celebrate the Passover were those who obeyed the word of Hezekiah, which was inspired by the Lord[cite: 5]. The mockers who refused to join Judah in Jerusalem did so because they refused to humble themselves (2 Chr 30:11)[cite: 5]. Humility and obedience, the characteristics that describe the repentant, are internally motivated[cite: 5]. Clearly, Judah repented because "the hand of God was upon them" (2 Chr 30:12)[cite: 5]. The phrase, "hand of God" is used throughout the Old Testament to refer to God's sovereignty[cite: 5]. In Old Testament narrative it often refers to God acting in judgment (Ruth 1:13; 1 Sam 5:6; 1 Chr 21:13; Job 19:21)[cite: 5]. However, it can be used in a positive sense as it is here in 2 Chronicles 30:12 (1 Kgs 18:46; Ezra 7:6, 28; Psa 118:15–16)[cite: 5]. In Ezra 7, as in the case of Judah's reception of the letter, the hand of God is a key motive behind the repentance of his people[cite: 5]. The remnant of the North did not repent, in part, because the hand of the Lord was not upon them[cite: 5]. There is tension between the necessity of the hand of the Lord and the need for human action[cite: 5]. Although, "the Chronicler posits an absolute moral responsibility...he knows that the attainment of this perfect 'one heart' or a 'whole heart' may be beyond human power, and both the individual and the community need God's help."28 The inability of men to return to the Lord on their own does not remove their moral responsibility[cite: 5]. God holds men responsible for repentance, and it is only by the power of God that men can repent[cite: 5].

The rest of 2 Chronicles 30 describes the God-honoring Passover celebration held in Jerusalem[cite: 5]. The result of the fourteen days of feasting (2 Chr 30:21–23) was "great joy in Jerusalem, because there was nothing like this in Jerusalem since the days of Solomon the son of David, king of Israel" (2 Chr 30:26)[cite: 5]. It was a truly remarkable celebration that glorified the Lord[cite: 5]. The Chronicler makes special note that the prayers of the priests came to God's holy dwelling place (2 Chr 30:27)[cite: 5]. Following the feast, Hezekiah led the people in an attack against idols and made arrangements for regular consecrated worship (2 Chr 31; 2 Kgs 18:3–4)[cite: 5]. The Southern Kingdom experienced a time of religious prosperity under Hezekiah greater than perhaps any before in the age of the Divided Kingdom[cite: 5]. The only stain on this religious reformation was the lack of repentance among the Northern remnant[cite: 5]. Restoration of the exiles was not achieved, because the remnant of them in the land mocked the Lord's call to spiritual and physical returning[cite: 5]. The ultimate restoration of the remnant prophesied by Moses in Deuteronomy 30 did not occur under Hezekiah[cite: 5]. The wicked remnant needed more than a letter from Hezekiah[cite: 5]. As the Chronicler made clear, the "hand of God" (2 Chr 30:12) is necessary for repentance[cite: 5]. The people needed a righteous king, who could physically and spiritually restore them[cite: 5].

The failure of Hezekiah's restoration should not discourage the reader of Scripture from the hope of the restoration ever taking place[cite: 5]. Hezekiah attempted and failed at accomplishing the restoration of the remnant prophesied in Deuteronomy 28 and 30, but Old Testament prophecy still anticipates the event[cite: 5]. Two prophecies in particular from Isaiah, who ministered during the reign of Hezekiah, express the same hope in the same language of Hezekiah[cite: 5]. Isaiah 10:20–23 contains perhaps the best concise summary of the restoration of the remnant[cite: 5]. After prophesying of God's judgment upon Assyria, Isaiah says, "in that day the remnant [š'r] of Israel, and those of the house of Jacob who have escaped [pĕlêṭâ] will never again rely on the one who struck them, but will truly rely on the LORD, the Holy One of Israel" (Isa 10:20)[cite: 5]. Isaiah uses the same terms to describe the people, š'r and pĕlêṭâ, that Hezekiah uses for the people he is writing to in 2 Chronicles 30:6–9[cite: 5]. Isaiah then writes that "a remnant will return [šûb], the remnant of Jacob, to the mighty God" (Isa 10:21)[cite: 5]. This return of the remnant is exactly what Hezekiah called for when he wrote, "sons of Israel, return to the Lord God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, that he may return to the remnant of you who have escaped [pĕlêṭâ] from the hand of the kings of Assyria" (2 Chr 30:6, author's translation)[cite: 5]. The return of the remnant was not a foolish dream of Hezekiah, but a reality that is foretold in Scripture[cite: 5]. Hezekiah's letter was an attempt to accomplish the restoration promised by Isaiah[cite: 5]. In this passage, "Isaiah is looking forward to a day when a restored Judah will have become wise enough to put her trust in God."29 The incomplete religious reformation of Hezekiah should be viewed as a type of the full reality prophesied in Isaiah 10:20–23[cite: 5].

Isaiah summarizes the restoration of the remnant in Isaiah 10:20–23 with the same vocabulary as Hezekiah's letter, however, he does not describe how the repentance of the remnant occurs[cite: 5]. The Chronicler made it clear in 2 Chronicles 30:10–12 that "the hand of God" is the difference between repentance and continued disobedience[cite: 5]. The operation of the hand of God in the restoration of the remnant is shown in another prophecy of Isaiah, which also uses the same vocabulary as Isaiah 10 and 2 Chronicles 30[cite: 5]. The glorious future of the restored remnant is described in the brief chapter of Isaiah 4[cite: 5]. The people in Isaiah 4 are "the survivors [pĕlêṭâ] of Israel" and "he who is left in Zion" (Isa 4:2–3)[cite: 5]. Isaiah is referring to the remnant who have escaped, the same characteristics of the recipients of Hezekiah's letter[cite: 5]. The remnant here in Isaiah 4 is the ultimate remnant who is said to return to the Holy One of Israel in Isaiah 10:20–23[cite: 5]. Isaiah does not begin by describing the remnant, however, but by describing their deliverer[cite: 5]. He begins this section by declaring, "[i]n that day the Branch of the LORD will be beautiful and glorious" (Isa 4:2)[cite: 5]. The Branch of the Lord is "God's eternal Davidic ruler of the people."30 As a prophetic title for the Messiah, the Branch of the Lord is used in connection with his salvation of Israel.31 In the day of the Branch, "the fruit of the earth will be the pride and the adornment of the survivors [pĕlêṭâ] of Israel" (Isa 4:2)[cite: 5]. A righteous Branch will produce fruit, and the fruit of the ministry of the Branch is the survivors or those "who have escaped" (2 Chr 30:6)[cite: 5]. The pĕlêṭâ cannot be the fruit of Hezekiah in his attempted restoration; they must be from the Branch of the Lord[cite: 5].

In Isaiah 10:20–23, the remnant is shown to be those who return[cite: 5]. The remnant of the Northern Kingdom in 2 Chronicles 30 did not experience restoration, because they refused to physically return to Jerusalem and spiritually return to the Lord[cite: 5]. In contrast, the fruit of the Branch, the pĕlêṭâ who is "the remnant left in Zion," they "will be called holy" (Isa 4:3, author's translation)[cite: 5]. The repentant remnant will be made holy since the Lord will have "washed away the filth of the daughters of Zion and purged the bloodshed of Jerusalem from her midst" (Isa 4:4)[cite: 5]. Hezekiah was not capable of washing the sins of the remnant of the exiles[cite: 5]. He consecrated the priests and the temple and opened consecrated sacrificial worship to God in Jerusalem, but the remnant refused to join in the consecrated worship[cite: 5]. The people needed "the hand of God" (2 Chr 30:12) to repent, and Isaiah 4:4 describes the work of the hand: he washes and purges the sins of the true remnant[cite: 5]. The removal of iniquity cannot come through animal sacrifice (Isa 1:10–16) or the kingship of a fallen man, such as Hezekiah, but through a righteous ruler who has the Spirit of the Lord (Isa 11:1–5)[cite: 5]. As the righteous ruler, he can be pierced and crushed for the sins of his people (Isa 53:5)[cite: 5]. A spiritual return to the Lord cannot occur apart from the kingship of the Branch[cite: 5]. The washing away of the sin of the remnant (Isa 4:4) will result in a new status of holiness: "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:6)[cite: 5]. The spiritual and physical return of the remnant under the righteous Branch will finally result in a holy nation[cite: 5].

The Old Testament is filled with calls for the remnant to repent, like Hezekiah's letter to the escapees of the Northern exile[cite: 5]. However, as the Chronicler acknowledges in 2 Chronicles 30:10–12, the spiritual return of the remnant (Isa 10:21) is not possible without the Lord changing the remnant's hearts[cite: 5]. Ezekiel prophesies of a day when God will give his people a new heart and pour out his Spirit within them (Ezek 36:26–27)[cite: 5]. The new covenant in Christ makes this heart change possible, as only the righteous Branch can purge the sins of his people (Isa 4:4)[cite: 5]. Hezekiah's aspirations, although well-intended, are misplaced[cite: 5]. His attempted restoration in 2 Chronicles serves as a type to point the reader forward, to the ultimate restorer of the remnant: the Branch of Isaiah 4[cite: 5]. The true Israel, the repentant remnant, will be saved and restored through their union with Christ[cite: 5]. The Servant, the same Branch of the Lord, looks ahead to the day of the remnant's restoration and says, "you will be called the priests of the LORD" (Isa 61:6)[cite: 5]. John sees a vision of the redeemed telling Christ of his salvation of the remnant, as he "made them to be a kingdom and priests to our God; and they will reign upon the earth" (Rev 5:10)[cite: 5]. The remnant of Israel, those who return to the Lord, will one day finally be to their God, "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:6)[cite: 5]. The remnant will not return on their own[cite: 5]. Hezekiah could not change their hearts and lead them in repentance and the Northern exiles never physically or spiritually returned[cite: 5]. Yet there is still hope for the restoration of the remnant[cite: 5]. Christ, who paid the price of the remnant's redemption, will be the one to lead them in their return to the Lord[cite: 5]. He will be their king and they will serve as his kingdom of holy priests in a consecrated new creation[cite: 5].
