In Heaven, on Earth, and Under the Earth: Christ's Victorious Descent

Christians everywhere affirm that Christ was crucified, dead, and buried, and on the third day rose again from the dead. However, the clause between Christ's death and resurrection in both the Apostle's Creed and Athanasian Creed is often overlooked: "he descended into hell." Believers should ask what the Bible reveals about the realm(s) of the dead, the state of the faithful dead before Christ's work of redemption, and Christ's burial and resurrection. To put it simply: What does Scripture say that Jesus did on Holy Saturday? This paper will articulate an approach to the doctrine of the descent, nuance and defend it against differing approaches and outright rejections and show the merits of this doctrine in the life of the Christian.

The Descent Defined

Examining the biblical presentation of the location and state of the dead sets the appropriate context for Christ's descent. The Old Testament presents Sheol as, in the words of Paul Williamson, the "realm of the dead, a place to which everyone descends at death regardless of spiritual status."1 Ethan the Ezrahite asks, "What man can live and not see death? Can he provide his soul escape from the power of Sheol?" (Ps 89:48). While the righteous are spared torment in Sheol, the wicked cannot be said to dwell there happily, as illustrated by the sons of Korah, who are swallowed by the earth and descend to Sheol alive (Num 16:30–33; cf. Deut 32:22; Ps 9:17; 49:14; Prov 5:5). "Sheol" can thus refer to the general realm of the dead, but also specifically describe "the realm to which the wicked go when they die under God's wrath."2 Matthew Emerson summarizes, "Sheol in the Old Testament and Hades in the LXX and in Greco-Roman thought can be used to refer to both a general place for all the dead as well as a place of torment or consignment for the unrighteous."3 The dual-compartment idea is best seen in Luke 16:19–31, where Jesus describes the rich man in the side of torment in Hades, and Lazarus in Abraham's bosom, separated by a great chasm. The biblical context of the realm of the dead sets the stage for the death, burial and descent of Christ.

Scripture teaches that Christ truly died, so His body was buried and His soul descended to the place of the dead. Emerson writes, "He experienced death as all humans do: his body was buried, and his human soul went ('descended') to the place of the dead. He descended to the righteous compartment of the dead ('paradise,' Lk 23:43)."4 Christ's death was a true death, prophesied in Scripture, as Peter in Acts 2:25–28 quotes from Psalm 16: "You will not forsake my soul to Hades, nor give your Holy One to see corruption" (Acts 2:27; Ps 16:10).5 David hopes in the resurrection of Yahweh's Holy One who would not be abandoned in Hades or see corruption but ascend to the right hand of God (Ps 16:11). Psalm 16 and Acts 2 demonstrate that Jesus descended, but did not remain in Sheol, as He was raised from the dead, body and soul, on Sunday. Jonah enacts this pattern of death, descent, and resurrection. He is swallowed by the great fish for three days and three nights (Jon 1:17), cries out to God from Sheol (2:2) separated from God (2:4), and views himself as brought up from the pit (2:6). His prayer to God is from the womb of the fish, and this "pregnant" fish vomits him onto dry land, giving Jonah new resurrection life.6 Jesus declares that Jonah is a sign, and as Jonah threw himself overboard to die, descended into the fish ("Sheol" Jon 2:2) and rose from the fish in three days and nights, so Jesus will be in the heart of the earth (Sheol) for three days and three nights (Matt 12:40). As John Woodhouse summarizes, "The primary meaning of the 'sign of Jonah,' then according to Matt 12:40 is... the correspondence between Jonah's experience in the belly of the sea creature, and Jesus' experience in death, his descent into Hades."7 Christ died, was buried, and descended into Sheol. God did not abandon His soul to Sheol or allow Him to see corruption but raised Him from the dead the next day.

Paul describes the descent of Christ through His use of Psalm 68 and Isaiah 14 in Ephesians 4:8–10: "Therefore it says, when He ascended on high, He led captive a host of captives, and He gave gifts to men.; Now this expression, 'He ascended;' what does it mean except that He also descended into the lowest parts of the earth? He who descended is Himself also He who ascended far above all the heavens, so that He might fill all things." Paul directly quotes from Psalm 68, where David rejoices over the ascension of the ark of God up Mount Zion and looks forward to the Messianic King ascending God's mountain in triumph (68:18).8 Christ ascended on high in victory but as Paul clarifies, before Christ ascended, he descended into the "lowest parts of the earth" (4:9). Some suggest these regions are simply the earth itself, in contrast to heaven, and that Paul is referencing the incarnation. However, "the incarnation interpretation appears to rely on assuming Paul in Ephesians has a two-tiered, not three-tiered, cosmology," and Paul explicitly includes all three tiers in Romans 10:6–7 and Philippians 2:10, where he follows the common Jewish, Greek, and Roman understanding of Sheol/Hades as the underworld.9 The "lowest parts of the earth" should be understood as the underworld—the realm of the dead.10 This is further confirmed by examining Isaiah 14, the other text Ephesians 4:8–10 alludes to, where the morning star who said he would ascend to heaven (Is 14:13) and make himself like the Most High (14:14) is caused to descend to Sheol and the pit (14:15).11 Paul draws a contrast between the morning star (commonly identified with the Devil), who sought to ascend yet was driven down to Sheol, and Christ, who descended to Sheol and ascended far above all. Whereas Christ follows the trajectory of Jonah in his descent and ascent, He reverses the trajectory of the morning star. Peter describes Christ's interaction with the morning star and his companions in Sheol.

Peter writes, "For Christ also suffered for sins once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous, so that He might bring you to God, having been put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the spirit; in which also He went and made proclamation to the spirits now in prison, who once were disobedient, when the patience of God kept waiting in the days of Noah" (1 Pet 3:18–20a). Here, Peter intersperses his discussion of Christ's death and salvation through baptism and the ark with a description of the descent, which is followed by the resurrection and ascension (see table 1). Peter clearly locates the descent within the chronology of Christ's death, resurrection, and ascension, paralleling the trajectory of Psalm 16, Jonah in the great fish, and Ephesians 4. He explains that Christ descended to make proclamation to imprisoned spirits and does so to declare "Christ's victory over demonic spirits after his death and resurrection."12 This is seen by comparing the text with 2 Peter 2:4–5, where Peter says God "did not spare angels who sinned, but cast them into the pit and delivered them to chains of darkness, being kept for judgment" and likewise did not spare the world of Noah (cf. Jude 6). Further, Emerson notes the only place besides 1 Peter 3:19 where prison (φυλακή) and spirits (πνεύματος) are used together is Revelation 18:2, where the referent is imprisoned evil spirits.13 In 1 Peter 3:19, as Peter Davids writes, there is seen "a proclamation of judgment by the resurrected Christ to the imprisoned spirits, that is, the fallen angels, sealing their doom as he triumphed over sin and death and hell, redeeming human beings."14 The descent then seems to mark not just Christ's humiliation (in so far as it is a consequence of death), but also the start of His exaltation.

Christ's descent is a victorious descent. Building on Peter, Emerson calls the descent, "the first of three stops on Jesus' victory tour: first he proclaims victory in the realm of the dead, then he proclaims it in the realm of the living, and... in the heavenly realm. In other words, the descent begins Christ's exaltation through the universe's three tiers: the underworld, the earth, and the heavens" (cf. Phil 2:10).15 It may seem strange that victory would be proclaimed through the descent, but not when the descent is read in connection with the resurrection and ascension. This is indeed how Ephesians 4 and 1 Peter 3 both present the descent. Christ is the one who can die and descend into the abyss and then rise and ascend into the heavens (Rom 10:6–7). Christ's descent marks an aspect of His victory, as the descent shows that He really and truly died. It is through His death that He defeats the one who held the power of death the devil (Heb 2:14). Thus, it is entirely appropriate for the dead Christ to descend and immediately proclaim His victory over the imprisoned spirits. Christ truly died and descended, which makes His rising and ascension all the more glorious of a victory. He proclaims "this victory, achieved through his death, descent, and impending resurrection, to all the dead, righteous and unrighteous, and to the disobedient angels. Christ's descent, in other words, is primarily part of his exaltation, not his humiliation."16 Christ defeats death through His resurrection and ascends as the victorious king into heaven. His "obedience even to death is the cause of His exaltation and His universal sovereignty not only over the heavenly and the earthly, but over.. those under the earth."17 The descent does mean that Christ really and truly died, defeated the demonic powers, and rose from the realm of the dead in victory. Believers are united with Christ in his death, descent, and resurrection, so as Christ was not abandoned to Sheol, neither will His people be abandoned. Instead, believers dwell in the presence of the risen and victorious Christ.

The Descent Defended

The church has held to a variety of views on the descent throughout history. Most versions of the Apostle's and Athanasian Creeds have contained the clause "he descended into hell" or "he descended to the dead ones." Different views arose in the church as to how to understand the creeds and texts such as Psalm 16, Ephesians 4:8–10, and 1 Peter 3:19. While the doctrine of the descent has not been formally denied in church history, differing understandings of it have led to a distortion of other doctrines, or a reduction of the descent into something less than what Scripture teaches. The following section will examine the salvific or universalist understanding of the descent and the notion of the descent as Christ's suffering on the cross.

The early church emphasized that Jesus descended to the dead as a natural result of His death.18 Justin Bass summarizes, "In the second century, the threefold purpose of Christ's journey to the underworld are already defined as a preaching tour, releasing the saints of the Old Testament, and a triumphant defeat of Death and Hades."19 Nearly all of the church fathers saw the descent as a rescue mission to set the OT saints free from prison.20 Many following the church fathers affirmed that Christ's preaching in the underworld (drawn from 1 Peter 3:18–22) was preaching not just in triumph over demons, or of victory to believers, but evangelistic preaching to dead souls. Clement of Alexandria and Augustine speculated in this direction21 and many within the Eastern tradition appear to hold to a sort of universalism tied to Christ's descent.22 The concepts of evangelistic preaching, a second chance for the dead, or any notion of universalism should be soundly rejected. Gregory the Great delivered a stern rebuke to a presbyter who drifted in this direction: "Hold ye nothing but what the true faith teaches through the Catholic Church: namely, that the Lord in descending into hell rescued from infernal durance those only whom while living in the flesh He preserved through His grace in faith and good conduct... For one could not be drawn to God after death who had separated himself from God by evil living."23 Note that Gregory does not deny the doctrine of the descent, but corrects erroneous implications drawn from it. The idea of salvation or a second chance for the unbelieving dead should be and has been soundly rejected throughout history. As Emerson summarizes, "Biblically speaking, a universalist understanding of the descent via either a postmortem offer or a complete destruction of Hades is not an option, either. The writer of Hebrews reminds us that, 'it is appointed for man to die once, and after that comes judgment' (Heb 9:27)."24 The descent must not be used to teach the salvation of the dead, in part or in whole. The preaching in Christ's descent is not to saints or to unbelievers, but to the disobedient spirits in prison (1 Pet 3:19).25 This should be understood as the proclamation of Christ's victory under the earth, a shout of triumph over the imprisoned spirits.

The Reformed tradition, following Calvin, largely drifted away from a geographic understanding of Holy Saturday. The Westminster Confession and the Three Forms of Unity all articulate an understanding of Christ's body being buried and his soul suffering the wrath of hell on the cross but ascending into heaven upon his death. Calvin understood the descent as "an expression of the spiritual torment that Christ underwent for us."26 Petrus van Mastricht describes the descent of Christ's soul "not into a place, but into the infernal state, wherein he experienced infernal things, or infernal punishments, in his soul."27 Question 44 of the Heidelberg Catechism follows this understanding, calling the descent "suffering unspeakable anguish, pain, and terror of soul, on the cross but also earlier." Van Mastricht and Calvin cite texts such as Psalm 18:5 and Psalm 30:1–3 that describe being in a state of anguish as being in Sheol. However, this does not necessitate reducing the meaning of Christ's descent to His suffering on the cross. This association of Christ's suffering on the cross with the descent was largely unheard of prior to the Reformation.28 The reduction of the descent to Christ's suffering and burial by the Reformed tradition largely stems from objecting to the Roman concept of Christ freeing the saints from limbo. However, the caution against limbo or a spatial harrowing of hell goes too far in reducing the concept of descent to suffering in the soul. The structure of 1 Peter 3:18–22 (see above and table 1) plainly divides the descent from Christ's death. There is a clear progression in the description of 1 Peter 3 and in the sign of Jonah: Christ died, then descended, then rose from the dead. Further, 1 Peter 3 and Ephesians 4:9–10 give no indication of Christ's suffering in the descent. Readers of Scripture would do well to follow the trajectory of Scripture in affirming Christ's death, burial, descent, resurrection, and ascension.

The Descent Applied

Scripture presents Christ's descent, not as a rescue mission or a second chance for the faithless, but as an essential part of His true death and the start of His triumph. In His death and descent, He defeats the one who held the power of death (Heb 2:14), entering the strong man's house and binding Him (Matt 12:29). In the realm of the dead He announces His victory over the imprisoned spirits (1 Pet 3:19) and He says now to His church, "Do not fear; I am the first and the last, and the living One; and I was dead, and behold, I am alive forever and ever, and I have the keys of death and of Hades" (Rev 1:17–18). The believer has no need to fear death or the powers of evil because Christ has triumphed over them. Because Christ died, descended, rose, and ascended, the Christian can die with assurance that they will be in the presence of the victorious Christ. If the descent and the rest of Christ's work mean one has nothing to fear in the life to come, then they can also walk confidently in this life. When the trials of life feel like walking through the valley of the shadow of death, the believer can be confident with David that Christ is with them, because Christ descended into the shadow of death. As van Mastricht writes, "Whenever our souls descend into the horrors of hell... what then is more effective than to think that Christ also descended, and that not for himself, but for his own, and just as God did not leave him in hell, so also, on account of him, he will not abandon them?"29 Christ entered the realm of the dead among those who looked forward to His coming, and they now dwell in His presence. If Christ did not abandon His own to Sheol, then He will certainly never abandon us in this life. There is no greater image of Christ's descent and comforting presence than His descent into the blazing furnace which caused Nebuchadnezzar to cry: "Behold, I see four men loosed and walking about in the midst of the fire without harm, and the appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods." (Dan 3:25). Christ who died, descended, and rose on behalf of His people still says to them: "Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name; you are mine... When you walk through the fire, you will not be scorched, nor will the flame burn you" (Is 43:1–2). Christ has defeated evil and death, walks with us through this life, and will raise us from the realm of the dead in the life to come.
