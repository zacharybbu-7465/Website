The Consummate Blasphemy: Antichrist in the Epistles and Revelation

Midwestern Baptist Theological Seminary · Kansas City, Missouri · April 30, 2026

Introduction

Christians throughout church history have always contemplated the end times, and the present age is no exception. The prophetic and apocalyptic texts of Scripture receive ample study, and perhaps even greater speculation. Among eschatological figures and events, one of the most significant is the person of the antichrist. Many possess, in the words of Kim Riddlebarger, a "morbid curiosity" towards this figure, and the antichrist and associated events have even garnered a stake in popular culture.1 Yet New Testament authors do not speculate about the person or timing of the antichrist. Instead, they foretell of antichrists and the final antichrist for the assurance of believers and warning of unbelievers. This paper will explore the teaching of the epistles and Revelation concerning the antichrist. First, it will expound Paul's lengthy treatise of the man of lawlessness in 2 Thessalonians 2, where he situates the man of lawlessness within a wider eschatological context. Second, it will examine 1 and 2 John, where the apostle shows that preceding the coming antichrist, many antichrists will deceive throughout history and provides guidance for escaping their deception. Finally, it will explore the teaching of Revelation concerning the beast, where the antichrist is presented in apocalyptic language and eternally damned, alongside the dragon and his fellow beast. While many antichrists have appeared and are present now, the final antichrist will be revealed shortly before the return of Christ. He will make himself out to be God and so deceive many unbelievers, while at the same time, persecute faithful Christians. He will be empowered by the dragon and through the deception of the false prophet, lead a final act of rebellion against God, but Christ will defeat him soundly in His second coming.

2 Thessalonians 2

Paul develops an extensive theology of the "man of sin" in 2 Thessalonians 2:1–12, building on both Daniel and Isaiah, as well as the Olivet Discourse (Matt 24:1–25:46; Mark 13:1-37; Luke 21:5-36). This discourse is situated within a resounding eschatological context encompassing both epistles to the Thessalonians. Paul wrote 1 and 2 Thessalonians to encourage persecuted believers. Their persecution had led to misunderstandings regarding the day of the Lord and the second coming, so Paul offers a corrective.2 He brings hope in 1 Thessalonians that those who have died will not miss the appearing of Jesus and its accompanying resurrection (1 Thess 4:13–18) and tells also of the sudden judgment on the unsuspecting world that will accompany this surprising day (1 Thess 5:1–10). By the time of his second epistle, the suffering Thessalonians are again alarmed, so Paul assures them that their persecutors will be judged at Christ's return (2 Thess 1:5–10). The reason for their fear is revealed in chapter two, as Paul urges, "Now we ask you, brothers, with regard to the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ and our gathering together to Him, that you not be quickly shaken in your mind or be alarmed whether by a spirit or a word or a letter as if from us, to the effect that the day of the Lord has come" (2 Thess 2:1–2). Sydney Tooth surmises the Thessalonians may have only "interpreted their sufferings as part of the tribulation they would experience right before the day of the Lord arrived" and feared for unbelieving friends and family that the time of judgment was now.3 However due to the phrase, "the day of the Lord has come" (2:2), including the use of the perfect tense (ἐνέστηκεν, "has come"), it seems best to take the false idea, as Beale does, to be that Christ's coming and the final resurrection were being claimed as already having happened, leaving no further future expectation of either event.4

The Thessalonians believe the day of the Lord has already come—including the return of Christ and the final judgment. This leaves them wondering why they have only received suffering and seem to have missed the day's promised blessings. So, Paul seeks to assure the Thessalonians that the day of the Lord has not yet been consummated. He writes "from the perspective of the second coming of Christ" and "focuses on the events in the world preceding the Second Coming."5 Christ's return is subsequent to the two events stated in verse three: the apostasy and the revelation of the man of lawlessness (2 Thess 2:3).

Paul states, "Let no one in any way deceive you, for unless the apostasy comes first, and the man of lawlessness is revealed, the son of destruction" (2 Thess 2:3).6 Paul's sentence is elliptical, but the point is clear: do not be frightened or deceived, for the day of the Lord will not come unless the apostasy and the man of lawlessness are revealed. However, he does not stop with this assurance, but details the coming apostasy and the man of lawlessness in the following verses (2:4–12). The man of lawlessness, empowered by the devil, leads an apostasy prior to the return of Christ. Paul seeks to demonstrate that while God is just (1:5–10), the devil is a deceiver, and many will remain under lawless man's satanic deception until the return of Christ for judgment.7 Those under such deception are the subjects of the eschatological apostasy.

The term "apostasy" (2 Thess 2:3) is used throughout the LXX and the NT to speak of a religious crisis facing God's people—a falling away from the faith.8 This apostasy is "the deception of unrighteousness for those who perish" (2:10), stemming from a deluding influence and false beliefs (2:11). The specific apostasy in view here appears to occur within the covenant community.9 This is in line with texts such as the warning passages in Hebrews, where Scripture describes those who are outwardly members of the church, but by their apostasy, show that they never had true faith.10 Joel Beeke summarizes, "Paul speaks of apostasy in the professing Christian church, though it will be part of a movement that will apparently draw in 'all' unbelievers (2 Thess. 2:12)."11 Christ describes this apostasy in the Olivet Discourse: "And at that time many will fall away and will betray one another and hate one another" (Matt 24:10). The apostasy is clearly connected to the second sign preceding Christ's return: the appearing of the man of lawlessness. So, the apostasy should be understood, in the words of Tooth, "as a final rebellion against God in which the followers of the man of lawlessness worship him."12 This second precursor to the day of the Lord receives Paul's primary focus.

The "man of lawlessness" (2 Thess 2:3) is a unique title to this text. However, his second descriptor in verse 3, "the son of destruction" (ὁ υἱὸς τῆς ἀπωλείας), is used in John 17:12, where Christ says none of the disciples perished, except Judas, "the son of destruction." The name marks Judas for judgment and here it corresponds with the judgment of the lawless man (2 Thess 2:8). From the beginning, Paul shows that the man of lawlessness is not to be feared, for his destruction is already fixed. Paul's goal is not to frighten the Thessalonians, but to assure them that Christ will not return before the lawless man is revealed, and that He will bring the lawless man to a swift end.

The actions of the man of lawlessness are chiefly described in verse 4, where he "opposes and exalts himself above every so-called god or object of worship, so that he takes his seat in the sanctuary of God, exhibiting himself as being God" (2:4). This "deceitful imitation" is "the most important characteristic of the Man of Lawlessness" and constitutes an attack on God and Jesus by claiming divine status.13 The man of lawlessness sets himself against and above both false gods and the true God. The goal of the man of lawlessness, as Geerhardus Vos emphasizes, is not to present himself as a Messiah figure or a great spiritual being.14 Instead, he desires to be worshipped as if he were God. Paul derives this teaching from the prophecy of Daniel.

Daniel describes a wicked eschatological ruler, and Paul draws on this figure in his description of the man of lawlessness.

Daniel 11:31–32, 362 Thessalonians 2:3b–4, 9–10
Mighty forces from him will stand, profane the sanctuary fortress, and abolish the regular sacrifice. And they will set up the abomination of desolation. And by smooth words he will turn to godlessness those who act wickedly toward the covenant, but the people who know their God will display strength and take action. Then the king will do as he pleases, and he will exalt and magnify himself above every god and will speak astonishing things against the God of gods; and he will succeed until the indignation is finished, for that which is decreed will be done. Unless the apostasy comes first and the man of lawlessness is revealed, the son of destruction, who opposes and exalts himself above every so-called god or object of worship, so that he takes his seat in the sanctuary of God, exhibiting himself as being God. Whose coming is in accord with the working of Satan, with all power and signs and false wonders, and with all the deception of unrighteousness for those who perish, because they did not receive the love of the truth so as to be saved.

Daniel's prophecy is clearly carried out to a degree in the desecration of the temple in 167 BC by Antiochus Epiphanes IV.15 Christ shows that the abomination occurs in the occupation of Jerusalem and desecration of the temple by the Zealots leading up to the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70: "Therefore when you see the abomination of desolation which was spoken of through Daniel the prophet, standing in the holy place (let the reader understand), then those who are in Judea must flee to the mountains" (Matt 24:15–16).16 Paul uses the language of Daniel and Christ in describing the desecration of the physical temple and destruction of Jerusalem to describe the actions of the eschatological man of lawlessness. He takes his seat in the sanctuary of God and exhibits himself as God, deceiving many into following him. This act is the ultimate abomination.

The man of lawlessness exalts himself as God in the very sanctuary of God (2 Thess 2:4). Beeke summarizes, "the man of sin will take a position of divine honor and authority in the professing church, and many will follow him."17 He is the man of lawlessness because by seating himself in authority in God's sanctuary, he denies the authority of Christ, the head of the church. Beale provides substantial reasons for interpreting the "sanctuary of God" not as a rebuilt physical temple but as the church: the apostasy occurs among believers rather than Israel; Paul never elsewhere applies "sanctuary of God" to a physical temple, reserving the language for Christ and the church; Paul has already used temple imagery for the Thessalonian church (1 Thess 4:3–8; cf. 1 Cor 6:18–19); and in Revelation the antichrist attacks believers described as God's "tabernacle—those who tabernacle in heaven" (Rev 13:6).18 In the words of Riddlebarger, "When Paul refers to the Man of Lawlessness sitting in the temple, he's referring to the church on earth when the apostasy occurs and when the Man of Lawlessness is revealed—not to the Jerusalem temple in either AD 70…nor to a rebuilt temple in Jerusalem at the time of the end."19

The man of lawlessness sets himself in the place of Christ in the church and declares himself to be God (2 Thess 2:4). This act is the ultimate blasphemy. As Stefanovic summarizes, "This anti-divine figure will occupy a place in the church displaying himself as God and placing himself in place of God claiming the worship that only God is entitled to."20 He deceives false professors within the church through "signs and false wonders" (2:9). Christ warned of those false Christs who would deceive through signs and wonders (Matt 24:24–27), and the man of lawlessness is chief among them. Paul "portrays the end-time activities of the lawless one in the manner of Christ. His revealing (ἀποκαλύπτω; 2,8) and coming (παρουσία; 2,9) mirror the revealing (ἀποκάλυψις; 1,7) and coming (παρουσία; 2,8) of Christ."21 Yet the "coming" of this man is "in accord with the working of Satan" (2:9). For this reason, Christ will destroy him in the true second coming (2:8).

Paul says the man of lawlessness is not yet revealed because of what restrains him (2 Thess 2:5–7). The identity of the restrainer, while a mystery in the text, had previously been told to the Thessalonians (2:5).22 While the restrainer is unclear, what is clear is the defeat of this lawless one, the son of destruction. He is the one "whom the Lord Jesus will slay with the breath of His mouth and bring to an end by the appearance of His coming" (2:8).23 Christ will demonstrate that He alone is God by slaying this imitator of God who tries to usurp His seat over the church. The man of lawlessness is judged by Christ, as are those "who did not believe the truth but took pleasure in unrighteousness" (2:12). Those who are deceived in the apostasy and worship the lawless one as God will be judged alongside Him. The second coming brings hope for Christ's own, but wrath for all who belong to the man of lawlessness.

Paul explains how to live through the tribulation in 2 Thessalonians. As John MacArthur writes, "Unlike much contemporary teaching on eschatology, Paul's motive was not sensational but pastoral. His goal was not to gratify curiosity about the end times but to comfort confused Christians. Therefore, he limited his instruction to what was necessary to correct the error that robbed them of their joy, hope, and peace."24 Paul's pastoral writing focuses on the antichrist, not for the sake of stirring up speculation, but for providing clarity and comfort. Believers are being persecuted, but God will repay their afflicters (1:6) and give full and final rest and retribution at the return of Jesus (1:7–10). Christians have not missed the second coming, as Christ has not returned to resurrect the saints (1 Thess 4:13–18) and judge the wicked (1 Thess 5:1–10). Prior to His return, the great apostasy and the man of lawlessness will be revealed. Although the lawless man will deceive many and blaspheme Christ, the Thessalonians do not need to fear him. Christ, in the revelation of His glorious second coming, will bring the man of lawlessness and all his wicked followers to a fiery end.

The Johannine Epistles

Paul warned the Thessalonians to not be deceived that the day of the Lord had already come. He also warned them to not be deceived by the man of lawlessness, who will lead a great apostasy prior to the return of Christ. True Christians are not those who believe what is false (2 Thess 2:11–12), but who should stand firm and hold to the apostolic tradition (2:15). John continues where Paul left off and writes to Christians who have an anointing from the Holy One and who know the truth (1 John 2:20–21). Like Paul, he warns them of a deceptive figure who opposes Christ, though instead of the name "man of lawlessness," John simply calls him the antichrist.

John's description of the antichrist opens with a time designation, "Children, it is the last hour" (1 John 2:18). The coming of Christ began this era of the last days, or the last hour (Acts 2:17; 1 Cor 10:11; Heb 1:2; 1 Pet 1:20). His life marks the start of the present age, the last days, as Christians look ahead to the age to come. John continues, "just as you heard that antichrist is coming, even now many antichrists have appeared. From this we know that it is the last hour" (2:18). The man of lawlessness, as shown in 2 Thessalonians, is a sign of the impending day of the Lord. In 1 John 2, the sign of the last hour is the appearance of many antichrists. John does not deny the existence of one ultimate antichrist—indeed, he says that his readers have heard of this person. F. F. Bruce cites 2 Thessalonians 2:1–12 as the basis for this knowledge, showing that the teaching of the man of lawlessness, or the antichrist as John calls him, was prevalent in the church.25 While John has in mind a final eschatological individual, he primarily here warns readers of the "many antichrists" that have already appeared (1 John 2:18).26 He describes these antichrists as those who were formerly among the church—as apostates: "They went out from us, but they were not of us, for if they were of us, they would have remained with us. But in order that it might be manifested that not all are of us [they went out]" (2:19). As the man of lawlessness arises from within the church and leads an apostasy in 2 Thessalonians, so John says many antichrists have appeared and gone out from among the true believers, showing that they are apostates.27 The apostasy of the many antichrists, climaxing in the coming antichrist, is a denial of Jesus.

Those who deny that Jesus is the Christ are antichrists, forerunners of the eschatological antichrist. "Who is the liar," John asks, "but the one who denies that Jesus is the Christ? This is the antichrist, the one who denies the Father and the Son." (1 John 2:22). Bruce comments that in the New Testament, God has revealed Himself definitively in Jesus, His Son, so that the Antichrist is so called because he claims for himself the honor that rightly belongs to Christ.28 The antichrist denies that Jesus is the Christ by claiming Christ's honor for himself, as Paul spoke of the man of lawlessness sitting in God's sanctuary, displaying himself as God, and doing so through signs and false wonders (2 Thess 2:4, 9). The ultimate antichrist will deny Christ through false imitation of Christ. This imitation of Jesus, a denial that He is the Christ, is the ultimate form of blasphemy. The core of the antichrist apostasy is present in any false teacher who denies that Jesus is the Christ. For this reason, John says many antichrists are already present, while the final antichrist is still to come.

The application of John's teaching concerning antichrists and the antichrist is stated clearly in chapter four: "Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits [to see] if they are of God, because many false prophets have gone out into the world" (1 John 4:1). The Spirit of God, and those who possess Him, will confess that Jesus is God incarnate (4:2), so "every spirit that does not confess Jesus is not of God. And this is the spirit of the antichrist, whom you have heard is coming, and now is already in the world" (4:3). The multitude of antichrists in John's day, and throughout the church age, are manifestations of the spirit of antichrist.29 For this reason, believers must test the spirits (4:1) so as not to be led astray in apostasy, either in the present time, or when the eschatological man of lawlessness is revealed. As the man of lawlessness deceives through signs and wonders and takes the place of Christ, so the antichrists leading up to him are deceivers who likewise deny Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh (2 John 7). Discerning believers who love the truth should not even welcome such false teachers (2 John 10–11). The reader should understand John's overall exhortation as a call to neither be deceived nor encourage the deceivers.30 This battle against deceiving antichrists is not done in one's own strength, but by the power of the anointing (1 John 2:20), the abiding word (2:24a) and the abiding relationship with the Father and Son (2:24b). True believers, as Riddlebarger says, will not fall away and will take up their sacred duty of opposing these antichrists with the truth.31 The antichrist, and many antichrists along the way, will deceive those destined to perish (2 Thess 2:10), but those who abide in the true Christ have a different destiny: "This is the promise which He Himself made to us: eternal life" (1 John 2:25). The antichrist, and all antichrists, will be judged by Christ, and they have no power to snatch away Christ's people.

Revelation

The Revelation of Jesus Christ shown to John displays the actions of the antichrist on a cosmic and apocalyptic scale. The purpose of the book is its unveiling of Jesus, demonstrating His greatness, primarily against the background of His second coming and final judgment. Those who oppose Christ, particularly false churches, the dragon, the beasts, the kings of the world, and Babylon, are described in detail. Judgment is poured out on each of them. The wrath and judgment of God on this wicked world is described as seals, trumpets, and bowls. God preserves a remnant through this time of tribulation, some of whom lose their lives as martyrs. Yet even in death, they come to life and reign with Christ. The outcome for the remnant is bodily resurrection to a new Edenic temple. The outcome for the wicked who align themselves with the dragon and beasts, is eternal death, not in a garden, but in a fiery lake. Revelation presents Christ as God, entirely unique, with none greater. The antichrist, empowered by the dragon and accompanied by the false prophet, is presented as a mere imitation of Christ. Jesus shows that He alone is worthy of worship by triumphing over the antichrist and all who worship him.

Paul declared that the lawless man's coming is through the "working of Satan" (2 Thess 2:9). Revelation likewise shows that the antichrist is empowered by Satan. Chapter twelve describes the great red dragon, with seven heads, ten horns, and seven diadems (Rev 12:3).32 The dragon sought to devour a child—Christ, in rebellion against God (12:4–6). He is defeated by Michael and the angels, thrown down to the earth, and now persecutes the woman—the people of God (12:7-17). The dragon's mission is to wage war with the woman's seed (12:17; cf. Dan 7:21).33 The dragon makes war with the woman and her seed through two beasts (Rev 13). The first beast, coming out of the sea, has ten horns, seven heads, and ten diadems, similar to the dragon (13:1). The dragon gives him his authority (13:4), and he in turn gives authority to the second beast who comes out of the earth (13:11–12).

The dragon empowers the beasts in their evil deeds, deception of the wicked, and persecution of God's people. The description of the dragon's persecution of the woman and her seed, along with the beasts, may rightly be said to describe the whole course of the age from the ascension of Christ to His second coming. There have certainly been both false churches and corrupt governments who have deceived the wicked and persecuted the true church throughout history, in the ways that Revelation 12–13 describes. Nonetheless, it is clear from 1 John 2:18 that John also knows and writes of an ultimate eschatological antichrist. The correspondence between the beasts in Revelation with the man of lawlessness in 2 Thessalonians indicates that John's vision is not merely a vision of false religion and persecution throughout the church age, but also of the ultimate eschatological antichrist, whom Christ will destroy in His second coming.

The objective of the dragon is to persecute the people of God (Rev 12:17). Persecution under the tyranny of the beasts stems from the first beast acting as the antichrist. As the beast is given power, a throne, and great authority, he suffers a wound that appears fatal and is healed (13:2–3). This causes the earth to marvel after the beast (13:3). As the beast's power and authority are derived from the dragon, the earth likewise worships the dragon and beast (13:4). The worship of the beast seems to encourage him, and the dragon gives him a mouth to blaspheme against God, His name, and the saints in heaven (13:5–6). This vision is a realization of Paul's description of the man of lawlessness exalting himself above gods, taking his seat in God's sanctuary, and displaying himself as God (2 Thess 2:4). This blasphemous creature is aided by a second beast who looks like a lamb but speaks like a dragon (Rev 13:11). He leads the cult following of the first beast (13:12) and even deceives through signs and miracles (13:13–14). Paul and John both describe these signs as empowered by Satan (2 Thess 2:9; Rev 13:2, 12) and indicate their purpose to deceive the wicked into idolatrous worship of the man of lawlessness—the antichrist (2 Thess 2:10; Rev 13:3–4, 12–14). Riddlebarger writes that the real threat raised by the dragon and his allies is not the sword, as frightening as that is, but its power to deceive and to draw people away from Christ and his church.34 All who are not deceived and do not worship the antichrist, but remain faithful to the true Christ, are persecuted. The beast makes war with the saints on the earth and overcomes them (Rev 13:7). All those who do not worship the image of the beast are put to death (13:15). Through the deception of the antichrist and the false prophet, the dragon deceives the wicked and wages war on the people of God.

The beast is the ultimate antichrist, acting both against Christ and his people, and in imitation of Christ. The antichrist and false prophet imitate Christ in at least six ways. First, the antichrist has ten diadems and blasphemous names on his head (Rev 13:1), a mimicking of Christ who has many diadems and an unknown name on His head in His return (19:12). He has the mouth of a lion (13:2), while Christ appears as the Lion from the tribe of Judah (5:5). He has the throne of the dragon (13:2), while Christ stands in the throne room of God (5:6, 12–13; 12:5, 10). The beast has a wound and seems to have been slain (σφαζω) but recovers, leading the wicked to worship him (13:3, 14). Christ is the lamb who had been slain (σφαζω), but is now alive forevermore (5:6, 9, 12).35 The false prophet features two horns like a lamb in this mutual imitation of Christ (13:11). Finally, the false prophet leads the wicked to receive a mark on their hand or forehead (13:16–17). Those whose names are not written in the lamb's book of life (13:8) receive this mark. In contrast, all who belong to the true Christ are marked with the seal of the living God on their foreheads (7:2–3).36 As Beale summarizes, just as the seal and the divine name on believers connote God's ownership and spiritual protection of them, so the mark and Satanic name signify those who belong to the devil and will undergo perdition.37 Those sealed by God may suffer at the hands of the beast in this life, but will receive their reward in the next. Those sealed by the antichrist may prosper during the dragon's war against the seed of the woman but will suffer the punishment of the dragon and beasts in the next life. It is the antichrist himself who unwittingly gathers his followers for judgment.

Judgment upon the beast and his followers is proclaimed by an angel in Revelation 14. Anyone who worships the beast and his image and receives a mark "will drink of the vine of the wrath of God, which is mixed in full strength in the cup of His rage, and he will be tormented with fire and brimstone in the presence of the holy angels and in the presence of the lamb" (Rev 14:10). All who apostatize, exchanging the true Christ for the antichrist, will suffer eternal torment. Revelation 16 describes God's judgment through the images of bowls, with the seventh bowl marking the return of Christ and the final judgment. Just prior to the pouring out of the seventh bowl, John sees three frog-like demons coming out of the mouth of the dragon, beast, and false prophet (16:13–14). These demons gather the rebellious kings of the world for war at the mount of Megiddo (16:14, 16). Finally, John sees a great harlot (Babylon) sitting on the beast (17:3). The beast is the same as the first beast in chapter 13 (17:8), and the angel tells John the beast "is about to come up out of the abyss and go to destruction" (17:8). That beast is the final in a long series of wicked kings (17:10–11) and he has ten kings who given him their power and authority (17:12–13). These are the kings allied with the beast (16:14) who "wage war against the Lamb" (17:14). John describes this same gathering of forces in Revelation 20, where the dragon parallels the beast in coming out of the abyss (20:3; 7–8). There, as the vision focuses on the actions of Satan, he deceives the nations (by means of the beast and false prophet) and gathers the ends of the earth for war, with their numbers being like the sand of the sea (20:8). "The kings of the earth take their stand, and the rulers take counsel together against Yahweh and against His Messiah" (Ps 2:2). The dragon and beasts lead the worshippers of the antichrist in a final rebellion against the true Christ.

The victory of Christ and His people over the dragon, beasts, kings of the world, persecutors, and all apostates, is summarized in this simple statement: "The Lamb will overcome them, because He is Lord of lords and King of kings and those who are with Him are the called and elect and faithful" (Rev 17:14).38 The dragon and antichrist have waged war against the woman and her seed, but in His return, the true Christ judges and wages war against the wicked (19:11), demonstrating His deity on the last day by judging the beast.39 His eyes are "a burning flame" (19:12), reminiscent of Yahweh's appearance in the burning bush (Acts 7:30), only this time, the fire does consume (Rev 20:9). He leads the armies of heaven against the kings who take their stand, and strikes down the nations, ruling them with a rod of iron, and treading the wine press of the wrath of God (19:14–15). This procession is truly a victory parade.40 The beast and false prophet are seized and thrown straightaway into the lake of fire (19:20), and the dragon himself who terrorized with fire is thrown in with them (20:10). The antichrist and his reign of deception are brought to an end in the revelation of the true Christ, the King of kings and Lord of lords.

Conclusion

The antichrist, in his coming, will deceive through the power of the dragon and signs of the false prophet. In the church, he will declare himself to be God, denying Christ's coming in the flesh. In the political realm, he will set himself up as a ruler in opposition to Christ and His people. The antichrist, with the rest of Satan's allies, will take his stand against Yahweh and against the Messiah in the act of ultimate blasphemy. The true Christ, in His coming, will slay the antichrist and the rest of the dragon's beasts with the sword of His mouth and fire out of heaven, and throw them into the lake of eternal fire. The teaching of the New Testament regarding the antichrist should not cause fear or alarm among Christians but spur them to be discerning of teachers within the church and political rulers. The church eagerly awaits the day of Christ's return when He will decisively judge the antichrist, and all workers of evil once and for all.
